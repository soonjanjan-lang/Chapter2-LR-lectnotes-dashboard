AI prompts for the literature review

A good prompt gives an AI tool a role, the context, a precise task, the inputs, firm limits, a format for the answer, and a check to run on itself. This lesson explains that structure and provides eighteen complete prompts for every stage of the review.

After this part you can

  • name the seven parts of a complete prompt;
  • explain why limits and self-checks reduce invented content;
  • turn a weak prompt into a strong one.

The seven parts of a complete prompt

PartWhat it doesExample
1. RoleSets the expertise and standards the answer should meet"Act as a sceptical external examiner for an economics PhD."
2. ContextTells the tool what it cannot know: your topic, setting, data, stage"My thesis asks how B40 households respond to food prices in Malaysia, using HES data."
3. TaskOne clear job, broken into numbered steps"1. Classify the gap. 2. Apply three tests. 3. List the five hardest questions."
4. InputsThe material to work on, clearly marked"[PASTE GAP STATEMENT]"
5. ConstraintsWhat not to do: the guard against invention and ghost-writing"Use only the sources. Do not add citations. Do not rewrite."
6. Output formatThe shape of the answer, so that you can use it directly"A table: sentence | source | verdict | quote."
7. Self-checkA test the tool applies before answering"Confirm every quote says what the sentence claims."

Weak and strong

Weak prompt

Write a literature review on minimum wage in Malaysia with references.
What goes wrong
  • Asks the tool to do the student's job, which is an integrity problem.
  • "With references" invites invented citations.
  • No research question, so the review has no direction.
  • No format, no limits, no check.

Strong prompt

ROLE: Act as a senior labour economist who writes survey articles.
CONTEXT: My question is whether Malaysia's 2013 minimum wage reduced youth employment. Below is my matrix of 18 studies.
[PASTE MATRIX]
TASK: Propose 3 to 5 themes that organise these studies; assign each study; note agreements, disagreements and likely reasons.
CONSTRAINTS: Use only the studies in the matrix. Do not add studies. Do not write paragraphs.
OUTPUT: One block per theme: name | studies | agreements | disagreements.
SELF-CHECK: Every study appears under at least one theme.
Why it works
  • The tool organises your material; you write the review.
  • Inputs are your own verified matrix, so nothing is invented.
  • The output can go straight into your planning.

One prompt, dissected

Prompt W4 from the library: "Stress-test my gap statement".

ROLE: Act as a sceptical external examiner for a PhD in applied microeconomics.

Role

"Sceptical" and "examiner" raise the standard of the answer.

CONTEXT: My research question is "..." in Malaysia. My gap statement is: [PASTE]

Context and input

The tool cannot judge a gap without knowing the question and the setting.

TASK: 1. Classify the gap ... 2. Apply three tests ... 3. Ask the five hardest questions ... 4. Point out any claim that is too strong.

Task

Numbered steps get numbered answers, which are easy to act on.

CONSTRAINTS: Do not rewrite the statement and do not invent studies that might already fill the gap; instead, tell me what kind of study I should search for.

Constraints

Keeps authorship with you, and turns a likely hallucination ("Smith (2021) already did this") into a search instruction.

SELF-CHECK: Make sure the examiner questions are specific to my statement, not generic.

Self-check

Reduces the stock answers that sound wise but fit any thesis.

Four habits

  • Give the tool your material to work on, rather than asking it what it knows.
  • Ask for critique and structure, not for finished prose.
  • Forbid invented citations explicitly, every time.
  • Treat every output as a draft for you to verify.

What journals and publishers expect

    
Publishers broadly agree on two points: an AI tool cannot be listed as an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work (COPE's position statement on authorship and AI tools makes this point), and authors must disclose how they used AI tools, typically in the methods or acknowledgements. Many allow AI-assisted language editing with disclosure. Check the policy of each journal before you submit chapters as papers.


    
A disclosure statement you can adapt

    

      
Example disclosure (for the thesis front matter or methodology)

      
Use of generative AI tools. In preparing Chapter 2, I used [tool name and version] between [month year] and [month year] to (a) suggest search terms and JEL codes, (b) draft a first version of the summary matrix from papers I had selected and uploaded, and (c) suggest language edits to passages I had written. I checked every entry in the matrix against the original papers, verified every reference, and accepted or rejected each language edit individually. No text generated by these tools has been included without my revision, and I take full responsibility for the content of this thesis.

      Teaching note: the viva test
      

        
Tell students that any sentence in Chapter 2 may be read back to them in the viva with the question "What did you mean by this, and how do you know?" A sentence they cannot answer for should not be in the thesis, whoever or whatever drafted it. This framing works better than lists of prohibited uses.
