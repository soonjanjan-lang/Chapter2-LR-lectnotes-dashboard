Searching the literature

A good search is planned, recorded and repeatable. This lesson takes you from a research question to a documented search across Google Scholar, subject databases, reference lists and AI tools.

After this part you can

  • break a research question into searchable concepts;
  • build synonym lists and use JEL codes;
  • set inclusion criteria and keep a search log.

From question to concepts

Search engines do not understand questions; they match words. So the first step is to take your research question apart into its main concepts, then list the different words authors use for each one.

Worked example: a concept table

Research question: Do international remittances increase household spending on education in Southeast Asia?

Concept 1: the causeConcept 2: the outcomeConcept 3: the unitConcept 4: the place
remittanceseducation expenditurehouseholdMalaysia
remittance incomeeducation spendingfamilySoutheast Asia
migrant transfersschooling expenditurehouseholdsASEAN
transfers from abroadhuman capital investmentIndonesia, Philippines, Vietnam

Words in the same column are joined with OR. The columns are joined with AND. You will rarely search all four columns at once: the place column is often dropped at first, because the best studies may come from elsewhere and still inform yours.

Use JEL codes

The Journal of Economic Literature classification codes describe what a paper is about, regardless of the words the authors chose. EconLit lets you search by JEL code, and most economics journals print the codes under the abstract. Codes for the example above:

CodeMeaning
F24Remittances
I22Educational Finance; Financial Aid
D12Consumer Economics: Empirical Analysis
O15Human Resources; Human Development; Income Distribution; Migration
J61Geographic Labor Mobility; Immigrant Workers

Tip: open three or four papers you already know are central, and copy their JEL codes and author keywords into your concept table.

Decide the limits before you search

Write your inclusion and exclusion criteria down first. Deciding them afterwards, once you have seen the results, invites you to keep the convenient papers and drop the awkward ones.

  • Period: for example, 2000 onwards, plus any older seminal work.
  • Document type: peer-reviewed articles, working papers from recognised series (NBER, IZA, CEPR, World Bank), theses, official reports.
  • Language: English and Malay, if you can read both.
  • Evidence type: empirical studies with household-level data; theoretical papers on the mechanism.
  • Exclusions: opinion pieces, conference abstracts without full text, journals that fail your quality check.

Keep a search log

A search log makes your review reproducible and saves you from repeating searches. Examiners increasingly ask how the literature was found.

Key takeaways

  • Concepts in columns, synonyms in rows: OR within a column, AND across columns.
  • JEL codes find papers that use different words for the same idea.
  • Set limits first, then search, and log every search.

Google Scholar: a practical manual

Set-up, Boolean operators, field searches, citation tracking and alerts, with a query builder you can use on your own topic.

1. Set it up once

  1. Sign in with a Google account. This keeps your library, alerts and settings.
  2. Link your library. Open the menu (three lines, top left), choose Settings, then Library links. Search for "Universiti Utara Malaysia" and tick it if it appears. Results then show a full-text link wherever UUM subscribes. If UUM does not appear, use the library's own eResources page and log in with your UUM Portal account.
  3. Show citation export links. In Settings under Search results, choose a bibliography manager (BibTeX, EndNote, RefMan or RefWorks) so that an import link appears under each result. If you use Zotero, install the Zotero Connector in your browser instead.
  4. Create your profile (optional). A Google Scholar profile lists your own publications and helps you follow other researchers.

2. The operators

Google documents only a few of these officially (phrase search and author:). The rest are undocumented but work in practice, so test a search before relying on it, and record the exact string in your log.

What Google Scholar cannot do

  • No truncation. remittance* does not find "remittances". Type the variants out with OR.
  • Long queries are cut. Very long strings are truncated without warning; keep each search short and run several.
  • No quality filter. Predatory journals, student papers and duplicates appear next to top journals. You must check every source.
  • Not exactly repeatable. Results and counts change over time. Log the date of every search.
  • At most 1,000 results can be viewed for any search. If you see "About 45,000 results", the search is too broad.
  • The "Cite" button makes mistakes. Its APA references often miss DOIs, issue numbers and correct capitalisation. Always check against the article itself.

4. Worked example: from too many to just right

Refining a search step by step

Search A, too broad. Every word can appear anywhere, so you get papers that mention remittances once in passing.

remittances education households

Search B, better. A phrase and an OR group for the outcome; the cause must appear in the title.

intitle:remittances ("education expenditure" OR "schooling" OR "educational spending") household

Search C, focused. Remove a common source of noise and limit the dates in the left panel to 2010 onwards.

intitle:remittances ("education expenditure" OR schooling) household -health

Then set Custom range: 2010 to 2026. Record the date and number of hits in your search log.

Search D, by journal. Check what the leading field journal has published: source:"journal of development economics" remittances education.

5. Follow the citation trail

  • Cited by. Under each result, "Cited by 312" lists later papers that cite it. This is forward snowballing; see Snowballing.
  • Related articles. Papers with similar text and citations. Useful when you have one perfect paper.
  • All versions. Often leads to a free working-paper or repository copy when the journal version is paywalled.
  • Search within citing articles. After clicking "Cited by", tick the box to search only inside the citing papers, for example for "Malaysia".

6. Keep up to date

  • Alerts. Run your best search, then click Create alert (envelope icon) at the bottom of the left panel. New matching papers arrive by email.
  • Citation alerts. On a key paper's "Cited by" page, create an alert to hear about every new paper that cites it.
  • My library. Click the star under a result to save it; label items by chapter section.
  • Google Scholar Metrics. Lists the leading publications in each field (choose Business, Economics & Management, then Economics) ranked by five-year h-index. A useful first look at which journals matter; not a substitute for the checks in the journal quality lesson.

Try it

      
Run the same search in Google Scholar and in Scopus or EconLit. Compare the first 20 results. Which important papers appear in one but not the other? Note the difference in your search log; it is a good argument for using both.

    

  


  
  

    

      
Snowballing: following references backwards and forwards

      
Keyword searches miss papers that use other words. Reference lists and citation links catch them.

    


    

    
The procedure

    
    
      
  1. Choose a start set. Five to ten highly relevant papers, ideally including one recent review or meta-analysis. A recent review is the best seed of all, because its authors have already searched systematically.
    2. 
      
  2. Go backwards. Read the reference list of each paper. For each reference, judge relevance from the title, then the abstract. Add the relevant ones to your library.
    3. 
      
  3. Go forwards. In Google Scholar or Scopus, open "Cited by" for each paper in the start set and screen the citing papers the same way.
    4. 
      
  4. Repeat with the new papers until a round produces nothing new and relevant. This point of saturation is your evidence that the search is complete for that theme. Wohlin (2014) describes this procedure in detail.
    5. 
      
  5. Log it. Record which seed papers you used and how many papers each round added.
    6. 
    


    

      
Example from the lecturer's notes

      
The reference list of Soon and Ahmad (2015) runs to more than 50 entries, most of them willingness-to-pay studies from many countries that were already screened for a meta-analysis. A student starting a thesis on household demand for green electricity in Malaysia would save weeks by snowballing backwards from it, then forwards through its "Cited by" list to find everything published since 2015.

    


    
Two more trails worth following

    
    
      
  • The best journals in your field. Browse the recent tables of contents of the two or three leading field journals (for example, Journal of Development Economics, Energy Economics, Journal of Health Economics) and sign up for their table-of-contents alerts or RSS feeds.
    • 
      
  • Key authors. Find the researchers who appear most in your matrix. Follow their Google Scholar profiles and personal websites, where new working papers appear first.
    •

Using AI tools to search the literature

      
AI tools are good at finding candidates and summarising them. They are poor judges of quality and sometimes invent references. Use them to discover, never as the source you cite.

A seven-step workflow

    
    
      
  1. Define the question yourself. Write the research question and concept table before opening any AI tool.
    2. 
      
  2. Brainstorm terms with a chat assistant. Ask for synonyms, related JEL codes and alternative phrasings. Use prompt S2 from the library.
    3. 
      
  3. Search a real database semantically. Put your question into Elicit, Consensus or Semantic Scholar. Save the candidate list.
    4. 
      
  4. Verify that every paper exists. Find each one on the publisher's site, through its DOI at doi.org, or in Crossref search. Delete anything you cannot find.
    5. 
      
  5. Check the journal. Run each new journal through the legitimacy scorecard.
    6. 
      
  6. Read the paper. Confirm the finding, the sample and the method in the paper itself. An AI summary is a pointer, not evidence.
    7. 
      
  7. Record the AI use in your search log: tool, date, query, and what you kept. Your university and your examiners may ask.
    8. 
    


    

      
The fabricated reference problem

      
General chat assistants generate text that looks like a reference: a plausible author, a plausible journal, a plausible year. Sometimes the paper exists; sometimes it does not; sometimes it exists but says something else. A single invented reference in a thesis is a serious academic integrity issue, whoever generated it.

      
Treat every AI-suggested reference as unverified until you have opened the paper. See fake citations for the warning signs.

    


    
How to verify a reference in two minutes

    
    
      
  1. Copy the exact title, put it in double quotes, and search Google Scholar.
    2. 
      
  2. If there is a DOI, paste it after https://doi.org/. A real DOI opens the article page.
    3. 
      
  3. Check that authors, year, journal, volume and pages match the article page, not just the title.
    4. 
      
  4. Open the PDF and find the claimed finding. Note the page or table number in your matrix.
    5. 
    


    

      
Ready-made prompts

      
The prompt library has complete, tested prompts for keyword brainstorming, Boolean string drafting, abstract screening and extraction into a matrix. Each one tells the AI to flag uncertainty and not to invent sources.

    


    

      Discussion: Is it acceptable to start a review from an AI "deep research" report?
      

        
As a map, yes; as a source, no. A deep-research report can show you the shape of a field in an afternoon and suggest search terms and authors. But it is not peer-reviewed, its selection of papers is opaque, and its summaries can be wrong. Use it to plan your own search, then build the review from papers you have found, checked and read. Never cite the report as evidence for a claim about the literature.

Screening and the PRISMA flow diagram

    
If your review has systematic elements, report the flow of records from search to final inclusion. The PRISMA 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) is the standard format. Free tools such as Rayyan help two people screen abstracts independently.

    
  


  
  

    

      
Reading efficiently, taking notes, and knowing when to stop

      
You will find hundreds of papers and read perhaps a hundred closely. Read in passes.

    


    
The three-pass approach

    
Keshav (2007) suggests reading a paper in up to three passes, each deeper than the last. Adapted for economics:

    

      

        
PassTimeReadDecide

        
          
1. Survey5 to 10 minutesTitle, abstract, introduction (question, contribution, main finding), section headings, conclusionIs it relevant? Which section of my chapter does it belong to?

          
2. GraspAbout an hourData and identification strategy, main results table, key robustness checks, figuresWhat exactly was found, for whom, and how credible is it? Fill in the matrix.

          
3. MasterSeveral hoursEverything, including the model, appendices and replication files; try to reconstruct the argumentOnly for the five to fifteen papers your study builds on most directly.

When do I stop searching?

    
The honest answer from the lecturer's notes is: almost never. But there are clear signs that the main search for a theme is complete:

    
    
      
  • You have read all the seminal works, the papers everyone in the field cites.
    • 
      
  • New searches and snowballing rounds return only papers you already have.
    • 
      
  • New papers you find cite the ones you have, and add detail rather than new themes.
    • 
      
  • You can explain the main debates in the field without looking at your notes.
    • 
    

    
After that, switch from searching to monitoring:

    
    
      
  • subscribe to the main journals' table-of-contents alerts or RSS feeds;
    • 
      
  • keep Google Scholar alerts running for your best searches and key papers;
    • 
      
  • join professional associations (for example, the Malaysian Economic Association) and attend their conferences;
    • 
      
  • offer to review for journals in your field: reviewing shows you new work before it is published.
    • 
    


    

      
Key takeaways

      
    
        
  • Read in passes; master only the papers your study depends on.
    • 
        
  • Write a note in your own words for every paper you keep.
    • 
        
  • Stop searching when snowballing stops producing new relevant work, then keep monitoring until you submit.
