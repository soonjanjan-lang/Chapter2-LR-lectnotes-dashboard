Writing Chapter 2: the literature review

These lessons cover the literature review chapter of an economics PhD from start to finish: finding and judging sources, reading them well, spotting the gap, choosing a framework, and writing a chapter your examiners will respect. Each lesson has notes, worked examples, tools you can use on your own topic, and exercises with model answers.

What a good Chapter 2 does

A good literature review is an argument. It does not list who said what. It shows what is known about your question, how we know it, where the evidence is weak or disputed, and why your study is the sensible next step. Every lesson below serves that one aim.

How to use these lessons

If you are a PhD student

Work through the lessons in order the first time. Use the tools on your own topic as you go: build your search string, keep your matrix, draft your gap statement. Tick each part as done; your progress is saved in this browser only.

If you are teaching

Press P for presenter mode: larger type, no side panel, and the arrow keys step through parts. Teaching notes sit in the purple boxes that open on click. The teaching plan maps the lessons to four three-hour sessions.

A note on examples: studies marked with a dagger (†) are fictional. They were written for these lessons so that weak and strong writing can be shown without naming real authors. Every other work cited here is real, and you should still check each one yourself before citing it.

The stages loop back

The numbers show a typical first pass, not a one-way road. Writing the gap statement often sends you back to search again. Drafting the framework shows you which theory you have not read properly. Expect to go round the loop three or four times between your proposal defence and your final submission.

How long does it take?

The final rewrite matters. A Chapter 2 written in year one and never revised usually promises things the empirical chapters do not deliver.

Teaching plan

For lecturers. Four sessions of about three hours each, with a tutorial in every session.

Learning outcomes for this module

By the end of the module, students should be able to:

  • design and record a reproducible literature search, including a Google Scholar search with Boolean operators and at least one subject database;
  • judge whether a journal and an article are trustworthy enough to cite;
  • synthesise studies thematically in a summary matrix, rather than summarise them one by one;
  • identify and justify a specific type of literature gap;
  • distinguish theoretical, conceptual, research and analytical frameworks, and build the one their study needs;
  • use AI tools for searching and drafting responsibly, and recognise AI-generated text and fabricated citations.

Teaching note: running the sessions

Ask students to bring a laptop and their provisional topic to every session. The tools in these lessons (Boolean builder, matrix builder, prompt builder, slop scanner) work best on real topics. A student who works on a made-up topic learns the steps; one who works on their own topic leaves with part of a chapter.

For session 3, collect one anonymised paragraph from each student beforehand. Put three of them, with permission, through the slop scanner and the rubric live. Students accept criticism of a peer's paragraph more readily than criticism of a published one.

Presenter mode (P) hides the side panel and enlarges the text. Esc leaves it. Model answers stay hidden until you click "Reveal", so you can pose the question first.

Assessment ideas

  • Search log (10%). A one-page record of databases, search strings, dates, hits and inclusion decisions. This is the habit that makes a review reproducible.
  • Annotated matrix (20%). At least 20 studies, with a "relevance to my study" column completed in the student's own words.
  • Chapter 2 section (50%). About 3,000 words, marked with the examiner-style rubric.
  • Gap statement and framework (20%). One paragraph and one diagram, defended orally in five minutes.
