What a literature review is, and why we write one

Before learning how to search and write, be clear about what the chapter is for. Most weak reviews come from a wrong idea of the task, not from weak English.

After this part you can

  • define a literature review in one sentence of your own;
  • explain the difference between a review and an annotated bibliography;
  • recognise the "product" and the "process" meanings of the term.

A working definition

A literature review is a critical and organised account of what is already known about a research question, written so that the reader can see why a new study is needed and where it fits.

Three words in that definition do most of the work:

  • Critical. You judge the evidence. Which studies are credible? Whose identification strategy is convincing? Where do results disagree, and why?
  • Organised. You arrange the literature by ideas (themes, debates, methods), not by author or by the order in which you happened to read it.
  • Why a new study is needed. The review ends by pointing at something specific that is missing, contested or out of date. That is your gap, and your study fills it.

The questions behind every review are simple ones:

What has been done?

Which questions have been asked, with what data and which methods?

What was found?

Where do findings agree, and where do they conflict?

How good is it?

Strengths and weaknesses of past designs, samples and estimators.

What is left?

What remains unknown, untested, or untested in your setting?

Product and process

The term has two meanings. The product is Chapter 2 itself, the document examiners read. The process is the reading, note-taking and thinking that runs from your first week to the month you submit. The process never really stops: new papers appear while you estimate your models, and the final version of Chapter 2 should reflect them.

Example: one literature, two ways of writing about it

Consider the debate on minimum wages and employment. An annotated-bibliography treatment would say: "Card and Krueger (1994) studied fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Neumark and Wascher (2000) re-examined the data. Cengiz et al. (2019) used a bunching estimator." Each sentence is accurate, and together they say almost nothing.

A review treatment says: "Evidence on the employment effect of minimum wages divides along methodological lines. Case studies that compare neighbouring regions find small or negligible job losses (Card & Krueger, 1994; Dube et al., 2010), whereas studies using payroll records and broader panels have reported negative effects for low-skilled youth (Neumark & Wascher, 2000). More recent work that tracks the whole wage distribution suggests that the number of low-wage jobs changes little after moderate increases (Cengiz et al., 2019). The disagreement is therefore less about the sign of the effect than about which comparison group is credible, and this matters for Malaysia, where the national minimum wage introduced in 2013 took effect across the whole country at once, leaving no untreated region to compare with."

The second version has the same sources but a point of view, a reason the debate exists, and a link to a new study.

Key takeaways

  • A review is an argument that leads to your research questions.
  • Organise by ideas, never by authors.
  • "Critical" means weighing evidence, not criticising people.
Teaching note: a five-minute opener

Show only the annotated-bibliography version of the minimum wage example and ask: "What does this paragraph want me to believe?" Students usually struggle to answer. Then show the review version and ask the same question. The contrast does the teaching.

Which one is Chapter 2?

In most economics PhD theses, Chapter 2 is a thematic narrative review with a documented search. You choose and organise the literature as an expert would, but you also record where and how you searched, so that an examiner can see you did not simply cite what came to hand. Some theses go further and include a full systematic review or meta-analysis as one chapter; that is a methodological choice to agree with your supervisor.

Example from your lecturer's own work

Soon and Ahmad (2015) is a meta-analysis of household willingness to pay for renewable energy. A random-effects model gave a summary estimate of about USD 7.16 per month, and a meta-regression showed that urban and North American households were willing to pay more than rural and Asian ones. The reference list of a meta-analysis like this one is also an excellent starting point for snowballing, because the authors have already searched systematically.

Review articles you should read early

  • Journal of Economic Literature: long surveys by leading researchers. If one exists on your topic, read it first.
  • Journal of Economic Perspectives: shorter, accessible symposia on current debates.
  • Journal of Economic Surveys: reviews and meta-analyses across applied fields.
  • Annual Review of Economics: concise state-of-the-art reviews.

Key takeaways

  • Know which kind of review you are writing; it decides how much search documentation you need.
  • A recent survey or meta-analysis on your topic is the best first read you can find.

Why we write a literature review

Seven jobs the chapter does for you, your examiners and your later chapters.

  1. Avoid reinventing the wheel. Find out what has already been answered, so that your contribution is new.
  2. Find the important variables. The literature tells you which determinants matter, how they are measured, and which controls examiners will expect.
  3. Learn from the strengths and weaknesses of past studies. Which data, estimators and identification strategies worked? Which were criticised, and why?
  4. Find the gap. Something not yet done; something done elsewhere but not in a different or unusual setting; or something done in a way you can improve.
  5. Choose and justify your theory. The review shows which theoretical lens explains your problem and leads to your framework and hypotheses.
  6. Set the benchmark for your results. In your discussion chapter you will compare your estimates with earlier ones. The review provides the yardstick.
  7. Earn credibility. Examiners judge whether you know your field. Boote and Beile (2005) argue that a researcher has to be a scholar of the field first, and Holbrook et al. (2007) found that examiners read the review closely as evidence of the candidate's command of it.

Worked example: what the review decides for a study

Suppose your provisional question is: Did the national minimum wage affect youth employment in Malaysia? A careful review answers several design questions before you touch any data:

Design decisionWhat the literature tells you
Key variableUse the "bite" of the minimum wage (minimum wage relative to the local median wage), not its ringgit level, because the same wage binds more in poorer states.
MethodCompare states or districts where the bite differed, before and after 2013 (a difference-in-differences design), and test whether trends were parallel before the policy.
DataHousehold labour force microdata (DOSM) for employment and wages; check whether informal work is captured.
Controls and threatsRegional trends, sectoral composition, and the spillover of wages to workers just above the minimum.
BenchmarkPublished employment elasticities with respect to the minimum wage, so that your estimate can be compared in Chapter 5.

None of these choices is yours to invent. Each one is justified by citing the literature in Chapter 2.

Try it on your topic

Write your provisional research question at the top of a page. Below it, list the five design decisions above and, next to each, one paper you would cite to justify your choice. Any blank row tells you what to read next.

Discussion question: "My topic is new, so there is no literature." Is this ever true?

Almost never. A topic can be new in its exact form (a new policy, a new dataset), but the underlying mechanisms have literatures. A study of the effect of a new e-wallet subsidy on spending draws on the literature on cash transfers, mental accounting, liquidity constraints and digital payments. The claim "there is no literature" usually means the search was too narrow. Examiners treat it as a warning sign.

Where Chapter 2 sits in the thesis

The review connects the problem in Chapter 1 to the method in Chapter 3 and the discussion in Chapter 5.

Common mistake

Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 tell the same story twice. If your problem statement already summarises ten studies, Chapter 2 has nothing left to add. Keep Chapter 1 to the problem and the gap in brief; let Chapter 2 carry the evidence.

What about a thesis by publication (three-paper format)?

Each paper has its own short literature section, so the thesis-level review is often shorter and sits in an extended introduction. It still has to show how the three papers relate to one body of work and what the thesis as a whole contributes. Check your university's current guidelines on publication-based theses before planning this format, and agree the structure with your supervisor early.
