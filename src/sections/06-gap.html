The literature gap

The gap is the hinge between what is known and what your study adds. A convincing gap is specific, evidenced, and worth filling.

After this part you can

  • define a literature gap and apply three tests to it;
  • tell a weak gap from a strong one;
  • explain why "not yet studied in Malaysia" is not enough on its own.

What a literature gap is

A literature gap is a specific question about your topic that existing research has not answered, has answered inconsistently, or has answered in a way that cannot be trusted for your setting. The lecturer's notes list three common sources:

Not done before

The question has not been asked, or a new policy or event has created a new question.

A different or unusual setting

A study replicated in a new country, population or period where the result could differ.

A better way

Better data, a stronger design or a better measure than earlier studies used.

Three tests every gap must pass

  1. It is real. You searched properly (databases, snowballing, Malaysian sources) and can show the gap with your summary matrix.
  2. It matters. Filling it changes what we believe about a theory, or what a policymaker should do. "Nobody has done it" is not a reason; nobody has measured the saving behaviour of left-handed accountants either.
  3. You can fill it. Data, method and time are available to you within a PhD.

The "not done in Malaysia" trap

A contextual gap is legitimate only when you explain why the Malaysian context could change the result: different institutions (EPF, price controls, targeted transfers), a different economic structure, different data, or a different population. Without that reason, examiners will ask "so what if it has not been done here?"

Types of literature gap, with economics examples

Based on the seven-gap taxonomy of Miles (2017), which builds on Müller-Bloch and Kranz (2015), plus two types that are common in economics theses.

1. Evidence gap: the findings contradict each other

Definition. Studies have answered the question, but their results conflict when viewed together.

Signals in the literature. "Mixed evidence", "inconclusive", "results differ in sign".

Economics example. Estimates of the employment effect of minimum wages range from negligible (Card & Krueger, 1994; Cengiz et al., 2019) to clearly negative for young and low-skilled workers (Neumark & Wascher, 2000).

Gap statement. "Because estimates of the employment effect of minimum wages conflict, and the conflict appears to stem from the choice of comparison group, this study uses variation in the minimum wage bite across Malaysian districts to test which comparison holds in a middle-income economy."

2. Knowledge gap: the research does not exist

Definition. The findings a researcher or policymaker needs simply do not exist, often because the phenomenon or policy is new.

Economics example. When a new targeted subsidy replaces a blanket one, there is no evidence yet on who gained and who lost.

Gap statement. "Because the shift from blanket to targeted fuel subsidies is recent, no study has yet measured how it changed the transport and food spending of households near the eligibility threshold."

3. Practical-knowledge conflict gap: practice departs from research

Definition. What practitioners or policymakers do differs from what research recommends, and the reasons have not been studied.

Economics example. Research generally favours targeted cash transfers over general price controls, yet price controls on staples remain common.

Gap statement. "Although the literature finds targeted transfers more cost-effective than price controls, the political and administrative reasons governments keep price controls, and the cost of doing so for low-income households, have received little attention in Malaysia."

4. Methodological gap: the methods used cannot answer the question

Definition. Existing studies use methods or data that leave the answer biased, imprecise or incomplete.

Signals. Only cross-sectional data, no treatment of endogeneity, only one estimator, measures that are poor proxies.

Economics example. Malaysian food demand studies estimate Engel curves only, so they cannot measure price substitution (see Methods literature).

Gap statement. "Existing Malaysian studies estimate Engel curves, which cannot capture substitution between foods; this study estimates a Quadratic Almost Ideal Demand System by income group."

5. Empirical gap: a theory's prediction has not been tested

Definition. A theoretical proposition exists, but it has not been tested with data, or not in the relevant setting.

Economics example. The "brain gain" hypothesis predicts that the prospect of emigration raises investment in education at home, so that a source country may gain human capital overall (Mountford, 1997; Beine et al., 2001).

Gap statement. "The brain gain hypothesis has been tested mainly with cross-country data; whether the prospect of working abroad raises educational investment among Malaysian students has not been examined with individual-level data."

6. Theoretical gap: no adequate theory explains the phenomenon

Definition. Existing theories cannot explain an observed pattern, or theories conflict and no framework reconciles them.

Economics example. Human capital theory treats migration as an individual decision, while the new economics of labour migration (Stark & Bloom, 1985) treats it as a household strategy. Neither alone explains why students whose families paid for their degree abroad often plan to return.

Gap statement. "Neither the individual human capital model nor the household model of migration accounts for the role of family financing in students' return decisions; this study develops a framework that combines them."

7. Population gap: a group has been left out

Definition. A population that could behave differently has been under-researched.

Economics example. Studies of retirement saving focus on salaried employees with EPF contributions; gig workers, smallholders and Orang Asli households are rarely studied.

Gap statement. "Retirement saving studies in Malaysia sample formal-sector employees whose saving is partly compulsory; the saving behaviour of e-hailing and delivery workers, who have volatile incomes and no automatic contributions, is unknown."

8. Contextual (geographical) gap: the setting could change the result

Definition. Evidence comes from settings that differ in ways that matter for the mechanism.

Economics example. Evidence that mobile money helps households cope with shocks comes mainly from East Africa, where mobile money spread before banks did.

Gap statement. "Because Malaysian households already have wide access to bank accounts, it is unclear whether e-wallets provide the risk-sharing benefits documented in East Africa."

9. Temporal or data gap: the evidence is out of date

Definition. The relationship may have changed since the data used in earlier studies, after a structural break, a crisis or a new survey.

Economics example. Household consumption studies based on surveys from before 2020 may not describe behaviour after the pandemic and the food price rises that followed.

Gap statement. "All existing estimates use survey rounds from before 2020; whether food demand elasticities changed after the pandemic and the 2022 to 2023 food price increases has not been examined."

How to find a gap

Gaps are found by reading systematically, not by inspiration.

  1. Read the limitations and future research sections. Authors tell you what they could not do. The "Future Studies" column in a good summary table (as in the Nigerian thesis table in the lecturer's notes) collects these in one place.
  2. Sort your summary matrix. Sort by country, then by method, then by period. Empty combinations are candidate gaps (see the grid below).
  3. Look for disagreement. Where findings conflict, ask what differs between the studies. That difference is an evidence gap.
  4. Look for new events and data. New policies, shocks and survey rounds create knowledge and temporal gaps.
  5. Read the research agendas in survey articles. Journal of Economic Literature and Journal of Economic Perspectives surveys usually end with open questions.
  6. Talk to practitioners. Officers at DOSM, BNM, ministries and NGOs know which questions they cannot answer.

Gap-spotting and problematisation

Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) observed that most research questions are built by gap-spotting: finding something missing within an existing body of work. They distinguish three kinds:

  • Confusion spotting: competing explanations exist (close to the evidence gap).
  • Neglect spotting: an area is overlooked or lacks empirical support (knowledge, empirical and population gaps).
  • Application spotting: a theory or method is extended to a new area (contextual and methodological gaps).

They argue that more interesting research comes from problematisation: identifying and challenging the assumptions on which a literature rests. For example, instead of adding Malaysia to the list of countries where the life-cycle saving model has been tested, one could question its assumption that households can borrow and save freely, when many B40 households are credit-constrained.

For PhD students

A well-argued gap-spotting thesis is entirely acceptable at PhD level. Problematisation is more ambitious and riskier. A useful middle path is to spot a gap and then explain which assumption of the existing literature makes it important.

Writing the gap statement

One paragraph in Chapter 2, one or two sentences in Chapter 1 and the abstract, and a matching contribution statement in the conclusion.

Although [what is known] (citations), [what is missing, conflicting or unreliable] because [the specific limitation].
This matters because [consequence for theory or policy].
This study therefore [how it fills the gap: question, data, method]. Four moves: known, missing, why it matters, what this study does.

From weak to strong: three rewrites

Weak

There is a lack of studies on remittances in Malaysia. Therefore, this study is conducted.

Strong

Although remittances are associated with higher education spending in many developing countries, most of this evidence comes from economies where remittances are a large share of household income. Malaysia is both a sender and a receiver of migrant workers, and remittances form a small share of income for most households, so their effect on education spending could be negligible or concentrated in particular communities. This matters for the design of education support for low-income households. This study therefore uses Household Expenditure Survey data to estimate the effect of remittance receipt on education spending among B40 households.

Weak

Previous studies used OLS, which has limitations. This study will use a better method.

Strong

Malaysian studies of the returns to education estimate wage equations by OLS, so their estimates are likely to be biased upwards by unobserved ability. Because these estimates are used to justify public spending on higher education, an unbiased estimate matters. This study uses changes in compulsory schooling rules as an instrument for years of schooling.

Weak

No research has been done on this topic, so this research is very important.

Strong

Studies of flood evacuation in Malaysia examine floods of typical magnitude, yet the decision to evacuate may differ when a flood far exceeds residents' experience, as in the 2014 east-coast floods. Understanding these decisions matters because evacuation plans are built on behaviour observed in ordinary floods. This study models evacuation decisions of victims of an unusually severe flood.

The last example follows the logic of Soon et al. (2018); see the annotated exemplar.
