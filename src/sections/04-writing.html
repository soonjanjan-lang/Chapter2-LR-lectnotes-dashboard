Writing well, and writing badly

The same twenty papers can produce a weak review or a strong one. The difference lies in organisation, synthesis and voice. This lesson shows the common mistakes, the approaches that work, and annotated examples from published work.

After this part you can

  • recognise the three most common weak review styles;
  • explain exactly why each one fails;
  • rewrite a weak passage into a synthesis.

Three ways not to write a literature review

The three examples below are re-creations. Each copies the structure of a published "review of literature" that the lecturer has used in class, moved onto an economics topic. All authors cited inside them are fictional (†). The originals are not named, because the point is the pattern, not the people.

1. The numbered summary list

Weak: one numbered paragraph per paper

4. REVIEW OF LITERATURE:

4.1. (Rahman, 2015†) had given the more elaborate definition of financial inclusion. According to him financial inclusion is the access of households to bank accounts, credit, insurance and payment services at an affordable cost, first by opening accounts, second by using them regularly, third by ...

4.2. Lee & Wong, (2017†) identified the possible areas of difference between financial inclusion in urban and rural areas. The different areas are on the basis of the distance to bank branches, the cost of transactions, the role of mobile money, ... They tried to stimulate further scholarly exploration of the exceptionally complex issues.

4.3. Ibrahim, Tan, & D Kumar, (2018†) identified the determinants of financial inclusion. Those determinants include Internal Factors (Income & Education, Age), External Factors (Bank branches, Mobile Network, Regulation), Personal Factors (Trust, Financial Literacy...)

What is wrong
  • Organised by author, not by idea: the reader must do the synthesis.
  • No comparison: nothing says whether studies 4.2 and 4.3 agree.
  • No evaluation of data, method or credibility.
  • Citation errors: the first author is in brackets as the subject, and there are stray commas before the year.
  • Long lists copied from the sources, with phrases such as "exceptionally complex issues" that say nothing.

Stronger: one theme, several studies, a judgement

Studies of financial inclusion now distinguish access from use. Having an account is no longer the main constraint in many middle-income countries; regular use is (Rahman, 2015†). Two explanations dominate. Supply-side studies stress the distance to branches and the cost of transactions, and find that inclusion rises when mobile money lowers these costs (Lee & Wong, 2017†). Demand-side studies point instead to financial literacy and trust (Ibrahim et al., 2018†). The two literatures rarely test each other's variables: supply-side studies seldom control for literacy, and demand-side surveys seldom measure distance. As a result, the relative importance of the two constraints remains unknown, which matters for policy because they call for different instruments.

Why it works
  • Opens with a claim about the literature, not about one author.
  • Groups studies into two explanations and compares them.
  • Identifies a weakness shared by both groups, which becomes a gap.

2. The definition dump

Weak: a list of definitions without sources or purpose

2. Literature Review

Green growth is highly regarded, but rarely defined. The most important definitions are as follows:

a. Green growth is a concept that encompasses all the economic growth and the interactions between environment and them.
b. Green growth including clean energy is differentiated from both brown growth and degrowth.
c. One agency defines green growth as a set of policies, technologies and investments which brings competitive advantage to the country.

As a result, measurement of green growth is important for two reasons: first, internal aspect with the goal of the most efficient dedication of resources while minimizing the costs. Second, external reason ...

What is wrong
  • Definitions with no citations: whose definitions are these?
  • No comparison of how the definitions differ, or which one the study adopts and why.
  • "As a result" claims a logical link that does not exist.
  • The section is called a literature review but reviews no research.

Stronger: compare definitions, then choose one

Definitions of green growth differ mainly in what they hold constant. A "strong" definition requires output to grow while total emissions fall in absolute terms (Ahmad, 2016†); a "weak" definition only requires emissions per unit of output to fall (Chen & Noor, 2019†). The choice is not semantic. Under the weak definition, most ASEAN economies have achieved green growth since 2010; under the strong definition, almost none has (Chen & Noor, 2019†). Because this study asks whether Malaysia's growth has been compatible with its emission targets, it adopts the strong definition and measures it by absolute decoupling of GDP and carbon dioxide emissions.

Why it works
  • Every definition is attributed.
  • Shows why the difference between definitions matters for findings.
  • Ends with the definition adopted and its measurement.

3. The bold-name laundry list

Weak: counting studies, then listing them

Research Gap In The Literature

The literature review is a significant step in each and every research process. The researcher has reviewed 68 studies, which include 50 Malaysian journals and 18 international journals. The various studies related to household saving have been conducted by different social scientists at micro as well as macro level in Malaysia and abroad. But no study was made on saving behaviour of e-hailing drivers.

Daud and Lim, (2016)† have conducted "Determinants of Household Saving in Malaysia". In this study, saving of households in 3 states are investigated. Age is found to be a significant predictor ...

Kamal Y. and Siva, (2015)† have identified "An Evaluation of Retirement Awareness Among Youth". This paper deals with the extent ...

What is wrong
  • Counting studies (and their journals' nationality) is not evidence of anything.
  • The gap is announced before any evidence is reviewed.
  • "No study on group X" is not, on its own, a reason to study group X.
  • Each paragraph starts with a bold author name and a title in quotes: a bibliography, not a review.
  • Verbs such as "have conducted" and "have identified" are used for everything.

Stronger: a reason why the gap matters

Life-cycle models predict that households save during working years to smooth consumption in retirement, and Malaysian evidence broadly supports the age profile of saving (Daud & Lim, 2016†). These studies, however, rely on samples of salaried employees, whose saving is partly automatic through compulsory pension contributions. Gig workers such as e-hailing drivers face the same retirement problem without automatic saving and with volatile income, two conditions that theory predicts will change saving behaviour (Kamal & Siva, 2015†). Yet no study has measured how much such workers save, or why. The gap matters because the share of workers outside the formal pension system is growing.

Why it works
  • Starts from theory, then evidence, then a limitation of that evidence.
  • Explains why the new group should behave differently: that is what makes the gap worth filling.

Teaching note: using the originals

The lecturer's notes include links to the three published articles these re-creations are based on. In a closed class session you can show the originals; on this public page they are anonymised. Ways of organising a review that work

Choose an organising principle that matches your research questions. Most good chapters combine two.

The funnel

Worked example: the same literature, organised two ways

Topic: determinants of household saving in Malaysia.

By author (weak)

  1. Modigliani and Brumberg (1954)
  2. Friedman (1957)
  3. A Malaysian study (2012)
  4. An Indonesian study (2014)
  5. A Malaysian study (2016)
  6. A cross-country study (2019)

By theme, funnelled (strong)

  1. Theories: life-cycle and permanent-income hypotheses
  2. Income and its volatility
  3. Age structure and dependency
  4. Pension systems and compulsory saving
  5. Malaysian evidence on each factor
  6. What is missing: informal and gig workers

Choosing your organising principle

  • Write your research questions on one page. If each question concerns a different factor, organise thematically, one section per factor.
  • If your contribution is a better method, add a methodological section that compares designs.
  • If a famous controversy surrounds your topic, give each side its own section, then a section on how the debate stands now.
  • Whatever the inner organisation, the chapter as a whole should funnel towards your gap.

Anatomy of a synthesis paragraph

Claim, evidence, analysis, link. Four moves that turn a summary into an argument.

MoveWhat it doesTypical wording
ClaimStates what the literature shows, as your own sentence."Evidence on X has converged on ..." / "Studies of X divide into two groups."
EvidenceCites several studies together, grouped by what they share."(A, 2014; B, 2016)" / "Using panel data from ..., A (2014) and B (2016) both find ..."
AnalysisCompares, explains differences, judges credibility."The difference appears to reflect ..." / "Because these studies rely on ..., they cannot ..."
LinkSays what this means for your study, or leads to the next paragraph."This leaves open whether ..." / "A second strand of research ..."

Worked example: a paragraph on mobile money and household risk

Claim Mobile money appears to help households cope with income shocks, mainly by making it cheaper to receive transfers from relatives. Evidence In Kenya, households using M-Pesa maintained consumption after a negative shock, whereas non-users reduced it (Jack & Suri, 2014). In Tanzania, consumption after rainfall shocks was protected for mobile-money users, which Riley (2018) attributes to remittances received through the service. In rural Uganda, users received larger and more frequent remittances and had higher consumption than non-users (Munyegera & Matsumoto, 2016). Analysis All three studies rely on household panels from East Africa and on variation in who adopted mobile money, which may be correlated with unobserved advantages despite the authors' controls. They also come from economies where mobile money spread before formal banking, so the effects may be larger than in countries with dense bank networks. Link Whether e-wallets have similar effects in Malaysia, where most households already hold bank accounts, has not yet been tested.

Synthesis wording

When studies agree

  • Several studies report ... (A, 2015; B, 2018; C, 2021).
  • This finding is consistent across ... .
  • Both A (2015) and B (2018) find that ..., although with different data.

When studies disagree

  • In contrast, B (2018) finds no effect ... .
  • Evidence on ... is mixed.
  • These results conflict with ..., possibly because ... .

Explaining a difference

  • The divergence appears to reflect differences in ... .
  • Studies using panel data tend to find smaller effects than cross-sectional ones, suggesting ... .

Pointing to the gap

  • None of these studies examines ... .
  • It remains unclear whether ... .
  • This evidence comes almost entirely from ...; whether it applies to ... is untested.

Try it

Take one paragraph of your draft. Highlight the claim, evidence, analysis and link in four colours. If a colour is missing, or the paragraph opens with an author's name, rewrite it. Tutorial T2 gives a full exercise with a model answer.

Annotated exemplars

Two published reviews by the lecturer, with notes in the margin. The yellow notes marked "Lecturer" are from the original lecture notes.

Exemplar 1: a debate between two schools of thought

Excerpt from Soon (2010), PhD thesis, University of Otago, Section 2.3 "Early brain drain debates". Citation format as in the original thesis, before APA 7.

The brain drain debate can be traced back to the 1960s when the 'brain drain' term was coined following the mass exodus of scientists from Europe to the United States. The brain drain phenomenon has generated a series of intellectual debates, generally concerning the effects of brain drain on the home (or source/sending) country. There are two schools of thought, the internationalist and nationalist.

Note 1

The opening paragraph gives the origin of the debate and then announces the structure: two schools of thought. The reader now knows how the section is organised.

The strongest proponents of the internationalist school of thought are Grubel and Scott (1966) and Johnson (1967) who advocated free movement of people across national borders. Migration reflects the free choices on the part of the migrants and is expected to raise total world output. The international circulation of human capital is seen as a beneficial process, unless the private gains from the migration are obtained at a social cost (Johnson 1967, p. 383).

Note 2

The topic sentence names the school first and its proponents second. The idea leads; the authors support it.

Johnson (1967, p. 384) went on to specify that the social cost is insufficient to demonstrate a loss to those left behind (TLB) or the non-migrants in the home country. In order to be considered a net loss, the social cost should exceed the private gains of the migrants plus any gains (or minus any losses) to the host country. Using positive externalities from basic research as an example, Johnson (1967, p. 386) argued that a net social cost (or a loss of world economic welfare) is unlikely to occur from skilled migration because the positive externalities generally outweigh the negative.

According to Grubel and Scott (1966, p. 270), skilled migration should be [ ... ] that two conditions are met, i.e., (i) the migrant improves his own income and (ii) a migrant's departure does not reduce the income of TLB in the home country. The first condition is met when the migrant moves as a result of his own voluntary and rational choice. This is because an individual is assumed to behave in a rational welfare-maximizing way. The second condition is met in a perfectly competitive market where wage is paid according to the marginal product of labour, i.e., when an individual migrates, he removes both his contribution to output and his income so that the income of TLB remains unchanged.

Lecturer

These 2 paragraphs explain the 2 theories in detail, separately.

"Went on to specify" shows the argument developing within one author's work. Page numbers locate each claim precisely.

Hence, in Grubel and Scott's perfectly competitive world where individuals are paid according to their marginal product of labour, there will be no welfare reduction either to the world or to the home country. In other words, the internationalists believe that the welfare of TLB in the home country would only be reduced if the migrants' marginal products or contribution to the national output were higher than the migrants' income (Blomqvist 1986, p. 165).

Note 3

"In other words" restates the position of the whole school in one sentence: synthesis after detail.

From within the same internationalist school of thought, Berry and Soligo (1969) however, disagreed with Grubel and Scott's marginal flow of skilled people, and had instead argued that for non-marginal flows of skilled migration, the welfare of TLB would generally be reduced. Berry and Soligo's (1969, p. 792) main conclusion was that skilled migration in general would cause welfare reduction to TLB as a whole, with two exceptional cases. The welfare of TLB will not be reduced if in the first case, the migrants own a relatively large proportion of capital stock and in the second case, the migrants leave some or all of their capital behind.

Note 4

Disagreement inside one school is shown, not hidden. The reader sees that the debate is not simply two camps.

However, to the extent that skilled migration can help relieve some pressures off tight labour markets in the home countries, then such a migration may not be welfare-reducing but may help in the human resource allocation. This situation can be deemed more of an overflow than a brain drain. This is the so-called 'overflow hypothesis' put forward by Baldwin (1970). Baldwin's overflow hypothesis is similar to Lewis's (1954) seminal theory of labour surplus in the agricultural sector migrating to the urban sector. In Lewis's two-sector economy, the move of the surplus labour from the agricultural to the urban sector does not have any adverse effect on the agricultural sector since the marginal product of labour is zero in that sector.

Note 5

Connecting a newer idea to a seminal theory shows command of the field and helps the reader understand it.

From the nationalist's point of view however, Grubel and Scott's purported frictional costs appear to be too large and harmful to the source country as well as too prolonged to be merely frictional (Watanabe 1969, p. 410). This is because, to nationalist Patinkin (1968, p. 103), the loss involved in the emigration of outstanding skilled manpower does not last only until they have been replaced; there may be a long replacement period that can extend over many years and furthermore, there may be a shortage of such outstanding skilled manpower.

Patinkin also disagreed with Grubel and Scott's claim that there is a negligible income redistribution effect due to the small number involved in skilled migration. To Patinkin, the question of redistributing income equally is almost impossible as there is no world government that can do that on an international basis (Patinkin 1968, p. 101). The nationalists maintained that skilled migration will reduce the home country's output by the amount of the migrants' contribution. From the nationalists' narrow point of view, the economic welfare of a country is weighed in terms only of the welfare of its residents, excluding both (i) the welfare of its citizens who have migrated and, (ii) the welfare of the outside world (Johnson 1967, p. 379).

Note 6

The turn to the second school is signposted in the first words of the paragraph, and the nationalist case is built as a direct reply to the internationalist one ("Grubel and Scott's purported frictional costs").

Exemplar 2: Malaysian studies, and limiting the international review

Excerpts from Soon, Kamaruddin and Anuar (2018), "Flood victims' evacuation decisions: A semi-nonparametric estimation", International Journal of Emergency Services, 7(2), 134–146.

There are only a handful of studies, in the Malaysian context, related to the current study. Padlee and Nik Razali (2015) studied flood victims' satisfaction with services provided at evacuation centres in the east-coast states of Kelantan, Pahang, and Terengganu. They concluded that flood victims were generally satisfied with the quality of services provided, especially in terms of food preparation and medical supplies. In another Malaysian study, Raman et al. (2015) studied how to best manage flood disasters from the perspective of flood victims. Specifically, their study proposed a disaster risk reduction model and recommended ways to manage and cope with flood disasters. Based on their proposed model, the motivation for preparedness in facing flood disasters should come from the people themselves, while rescue agencies and emergency services should focus primarily on ensuring the safety of vulnerable segments of communities and mechanisms for information dissemination. In fact, for disaster information dissemination and management Malaysia has already put in place a web-based integrated community emergency management and awareness system, although the system is still at the prototype stage (Dorasamy et al., 2017; Skar et al., 2016; Raman et al., 2014). Rahman's (2012) work represents a qualitative study on how knowledge of the NSC's Directive 20 amongst various disaster-related agencies in Malaysia's state of Kedah could translate into preparedness in the event of actual disasters. He came to the conclusion that knowledge of the directive did indeed translate into better preparedness for disasters, not only in the event of floods but also landslides, tsunamis, industrial pollution, and droughts. All the aforementioned Malaysian studies are done in the context of floods of typical magnitude.

Lecturer

This para is under the theme "Malaysian studies".

"A handful" means only 3 studies.

The last sentence in the para sums up the studies' common feature, which is also the gap: none studied floods of unusual magnitude.

Flood disasters in Malaysia are distinctly different from the natural disasters in some other geographical regions. In the USA for example, flood-related disasters are usually caused by hurricanes. In Malaysia, floods are often caused by heavy incessant tropical rains. Since the international literature is replete with studies on natural disasters, only relatively recent studies (both on actual behaviour and on hypothetical expected behaviour) of the determinants of evacuation decisions or behaviour at the household level are reviewed here. Thompson et al. (2017) provided an excellent systematic review of the much broader international literature concerning evacuation from natural disasters.

Lecturer

This para links Malaysian studies with those from other countries, limits the scope of the review of international studies, and refers readers elsewhere if they want to know more.

Solis et al. (2010) and Mesa-Arango et al. (2013) studied evacuation choices of hurricane disaster victims in the USA. Using a simple probit model, Solis et al. concluded that households affected by hurricanes were more likely to evacuate than to stay if: they were living in mobile homes, they were in flood-prone areas, they had experienced some form of hurricane disaster threat in the past, and they had young children. Mesa-Arango et al., on the other hand, examined types of evacuation destination using a nested logit model, rather than analysing the binary yes-no evacuation decision. Low-income households were found to be more likely to evacuate to public shelters; households that received evacuation notices in advance were more inclined to move to hotels; and households that had experienced past hurricanes tended to stay with relatives and friends.

Lecturer

The phrase "on the other hand" links the two studies by contrasting their use of different models.

[...] Mozumder et al. highlighted how different types of evacuation orders from the authorities result in different evacuation outcomes. Their findings were largely congruent with previous studies (Dixit et al., 2012; Petrolia and Bhattacharjee, 2010; Fu et al., 2007), with victims of natural disasters exhibiting more positive evacuation behaviour if mandatory evacuation orders are issued compared to voluntary evacuation notices.

Note

One study is placed among others: "largely congruent with" signals agreement and lets three further studies be cited in one clause.

What the exemplars have in common

  • Each paragraph has one job, announced in its first sentence.
  • Linking phrases ("went on to", "in other words", "however", "on the other hand", "largely congruent with") carry the argument from study to study.
  • Each section ends by drawing out what the studies share, which prepares the gap.

Tense

  • Present simple for established knowledge and for what a text argues: "Human capital theory predicts that ..."; "Becker (1968) argues that ...".
  • Past simple for what a specific study did and found: "Jack and Suri (2014) found that ...".
  • Present perfect for a body of research up to now: "Several studies have examined ...". Use it to open a paragraph about a whole strand.

Hedging: claim exactly as much as the evidence allows

Overclaiming

These studies prove that remittances increase education spending.

Calibrated

These studies suggest that remittances are associated with higher education spending, although only two address the endogeneity of migration.

Signposting

  • Opening a section: This section reviews ... . It is organised in three parts.
  • Moving on: A second strand of research ... / Turning to the Malaysian evidence, ...
  • Summing up: Taken together, these studies indicate ... .
  • Narrowing: Of particular relevance to the present study is ... .

For a much larger bank of phrases, see the Academic Phrasebank from the University of Manchester. Use it for structure, then put the content in your own words.

APA 7 essentials for Chapter 2

The rules you will use every day. Your reference manager does most of this for you, but check the output.

References versus bibliography

You cite it  =  it goes in your References.
You only read it  =  it belongs in a Bibliography, which APA does not use. APA 7 asks for a reference list: every work cited in the text, and nothing else. Every in-text citation must appear in the list, and every entry in the list must be cited.

In-text citations

CaseParentheticalNarrative
One author(Becker, 1968)Becker (1968)
Two authors(Grubel & Scott, 1966)Grubel and Scott (1966)
Three or more authors (from the first citation)(Cengiz et al., 2019)Cengiz et al. (2019)
Group author, first then later citations(Department of Statistics Malaysia [DOSM], 2023), then (DOSM, 2023)Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM, 2023), then DOSM (2023)
Several works, one parenthesis(Card & Krueger, 1994; Dube et al., 2010)Alphabetical order, separated by semicolons
Direct quotation(Johnson, 1967, p. 383)Johnson (1967) argued that "..." (p. 383)
Secondary source (use sparingly)(Patinkin, 1968, as cited in Soon, 2010)Only Soon (2010) goes in your reference list

Mix the two forms. Narrative citations put the author in focus ("Becker (1968) argues ..."); parenthetical citations keep the idea in focus ("Crime responds to expected punishment (Becker, 1968)"). A good review mostly keeps the ideas in focus.

The errors that cost marks

  • In-text citations with no entry in the reference list, or the reverse.
  • Different years for the same work in the text and the list.
  • "&" inside a narrative citation ("Grubel & Scott (1966) argue"): use "and".
  • Article titles in Title Case: APA uses sentence case for article titles.
  • DOIs as "doi:10..." or "Retrieved from": APA 7 uses https://doi.org/... with no label.
  • References copied from Google Scholar's "Cite" button without checking.
