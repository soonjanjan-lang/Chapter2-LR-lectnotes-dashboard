Summary matrix and mind map

Two tools that turn a pile of papers into a structure. The matrix lets you compare studies across the same attributes; the mind map shows how the themes connect. Both can be started with Gemini Notebook, and both must be finished by you.

After this part you can

  • distinguish a summary table from a synthesis matrix;
  • choose columns for empirical and theoretical studies in economics;
  • present a summary table properly in your thesis.

Two kinds of matrix

Summary table

Rows: studies. Columns: attributes (country, data, method, findings).

Appears in the thesis, usually as "Table 2.x: Summary of empirical studies". Shows the reader the evidence at a glance and lets them see patterns in data and methods.

Synthesis matrix

Rows: studies. Columns: your themes or sub-questions.

A private writing tool. Each cell says what a study contributes to a theme. Read down a column and you have the material for one thematic paragraph.

For theoretical works use: theory or model, authors, core proposition, key assumptions, predictions, critiques, and the variables it implies. For a meta-analysis add effect size, standard error and a quality score.

Example 2: a column design to improve

The lecture notes also show a summary table from another thesis with these columns:

S/NoAuthor/YearTitleObjectiveCountryVariablesMethodologyFindingsFuture Studies
What would you keep, drop or add?
  • Keep Country, Variables, Methodology and Findings, and especially Future Studies, which collects the authors' own suggestions and is a ready source of gaps.
  • Drop Title and Objective: they repeat each other, take a third of the width, and belong in the reference list.
  • Add Data and sample (period, N), the identification strategy, magnitudes in the Findings column, and your own Limitations column.

Presenting the summary table in your thesis

  • Refer to it in the text ("Table 2.3 summarises ...") and draw a conclusion from it; never let the table replace your synthesis.
  • Order rows by theme, then by year, not by whatever order you read the papers.
  • Use landscape pages and a smaller font if needed; long tables can go in an appendix with a short version in the chapter.
  • Keep each cell to a phrase. If a cell needs a paragraph, the paragraph belongs in the text.

Building a summary table with Gemini Notebook

Gemini Notebook is the new name (since July 2026) of Google's NotebookLM. It still lives at notebooklm.google, and it answers only from the sources you upload, with citations.

Why this tool suits the matrix

General chatbots summarise papers from memory and may invent details. Gemini Notebook works from the PDFs you give it and links each statement to the passage it came from, so you can check every cell. Its Data Table output, in the Studio panel, builds a structured table from your sources and exports it to Google Sheets.

Step by step

  1. Open notebooklm.google and sign in with a Google account. Create a new notebook and name it after one section of your chapter, for example "2.4 Price elasticities".
  2. Add your sources. Upload the PDFs of the papers for that section (one theme per notebook works best). Use text-based PDFs; scanned pages without text recognition are read poorly. Name files Author_Year.pdf so citations are easy to read.
  3. Check every source loaded. Click each source to see its summary. Remove any that failed or are duplicates. Notebooks have a limit on the number of sources, which depends on your plan.
  4. Create the data table. In the Studio panel on the right, choose Data Table. Select the pencil icon to customise it, and describe the rows and columns you want; paste the prompt below. Choose the output language.
  5. Verify every cell. Open the table and click the citations. Check each figure, sample size and finding against the paper. Correct anything wrong; note anything the tool could not find.
  6. Export. Open the three-dot menu next to the table and choose Export to Sheets. The table goes into the first tab of a new Google Sheet, and the citations into the second tab.
  7. Add your judgement. In Sheets, fill the columns no tool can fill properly: your assessment of credibility, the journal check, and the relevance to your study.
  8. Bring it home. Download the sheet as CSV or Excel, or paste rows into the matrix builder, and keep it with your Zotero library.
Data Table prompt (paste into the customise box)
Create a table with one row for each empirical study in my sources. Use exactly these columns:
1. Author(s) and year (APA in-text style, e.g. "Jack & Suri (2014)")
2. Country or region
3. Period of the data
4. Data source and sample size (unit of observation and N)
5. Dependent variable, as the authors measure it
6. Key explanatory variables
7. Estimation method
8. Identification strategy (what the authors rely on to claim a causal effect; write "none stated" if there is none)
9. Main finding, with the size of the effect and the table or page number where it is reported
10. Limitations stated by the authors

Rules:
- Use only information that appears in the sources. If a source does not report something, write "not reported". Do not infer or estimate.
- Keep each cell under 30 words.
- Exclude sources that are reviews, editorials or theoretical papers, and list their titles below the table.

Limits to keep in mind

  • It can misread numbers. Regression tables, especially in scanned PDFs, are a common source of errors. Check every coefficient you will cite.
  • It knows only what you upload. A table built from twelve papers is not a review of the field.
  • It does not judge quality. It will summarise a predatory journal's paper as confidently as an AER paper.
  • Copyright and confidentiality. Check that your library's licence terms allow uploading subscribed PDFs to a cloud service, and never upload confidential data or unpublished work you do not own.
  • Features depend on your plan. Google rolled Data Tables out to paid plans first and to free users afterwards; the menus may change. If Data Table is missing, ask for the table in the chat, save the answer as a note, then export the note to Google Docs.
Teaching note: a 20-minute lab

Give each pair of students the same five open-access PDFs. Half build the matrix by hand from the abstracts and tables; half use Gemini Notebook with the prompt above. Then swap and verify each other's tables against the papers. The discussion that follows (which errors did the tool make, and which did the humans make?) teaches verification better than any warning.

The literature review mind map

A picture of how the themes of your review connect. Draw it early to plan the chapter; redraw it late to check that the chapter still matches.

A mind map places your topic at the centre, the main themes as branches, and sub-themes, theories and key studies as twigs. It is useful for seeing which themes are thin, where theories connect to evidence, and how to order the chapter sections. Click a node to fold or unfold its branch; scroll or pinch to zoom.

Mind map drawn with the free markmap library from a plain-text outline. It is based on the structure of Soon (2010), Chapter 2.

Text version of the mind map
  • Theories: human capital theory of migration (Sjaastad, 1962; Harris & Todaro, 1970); random utility maximisation; new economics of labour migration (Stark & Bloom, 1985).
  • Debates: internationalist school (Grubel & Scott, 1966; Johnson, 1967; Baldwin's overflow hypothesis, 1970); nationalist school (Patinkin, 1968; Watanabe, 1969); brain gain hypothesis (Mountford, 1997; Beine et al., 2001).
  • Determinants of non-return: economic, family and social, education, political.
  • Data and methods: intention surveys, visa records; OLS and ANOVA in early studies, then logit, probit and ordered models.
  • Gap: intentions of current students; Malaysia as the home country.

From mind map to chapter outline

  1. Each main branch becomes a section of Chapter 2 (here: theories, debates, determinants, data and methods).
  2. Order the sections so that the chapter funnels: theory first, the gap last.
  3. Thin branches show where to read more before you write.
  4. Cross-links between branches (a theory that predicts a determinant) become the explanations in your framework.

Tools for drawing your own

  • Pen and paper, or a whiteboard: fastest for the first version.
  • markmap: type an indented outline and get an interactive map, as above. Free.
  • XMind, Miro, draw.io: drag-and-drop mind maps with export to image or PDF.
  • Gemini Notebook: generates a map from your uploaded sources; see the next part.

Creating a mind map with Gemini Notebook

Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM) draws a branching map of the main topics in your sources. Use it to check your own map, not to replace it.

Step by step

  1. Prepare the notebook. Open (or create) a notebook at notebooklm.google containing the papers for your whole chapter, or for one large theme.
  2. Choose the sources to map. In the Sources panel, tick only the sources you want included. Mapping five core papers gives a clearer map than mapping fifty.
  3. Generate the map. Select the Mind Map chip in the chat area. After a short wait, a new note containing the mind map appears in the Studio panel.
  4. Explore it. Open the map. Use the controls at the top right to expand or collapse all branches; zoom and scroll to move around. Expand branches to see sub-topics.
  5. Ask about a node. Select any node and Gemini Notebook asks about that topic in the chat, answering with citations to your sources. This is the most useful feature: it takes you from a theme straight to the passages that support it.
  6. Download it. Select Download in the mind map window. The map is saved as an image (PNG) you can put in your notes or slides.
  7. Regenerate if needed. To try again with different sources, open the note's More menu (three dots), choose Delete, change the ticked sources, and generate again.
  8. Compare with your own map. Which themes did the tool find that you missed? Which of your themes did it miss, and is that because the uploaded papers do not cover them?
Follow-up prompt in the chat: from map to outline
Using only the selected sources, propose an outline for the literature review chapter of a PhD thesis on [your topic].
- Organise it thematically and in a funnel: theory first, then international evidence, then evidence from [Malaysia / your setting], ending with what remains unknown.
- For each section, list the sources that support it, with author and year.
- Under each section, note any disagreement between sources.
- At the end, list topics that seem important but are covered by fewer than two sources.
Return the outline as an indented list in plain text, so that I can paste it into a mind-map tool.

The indented list it returns can be pasted into the markmap editor, where you can edit nodes freely, which the built-in map does not allow.

Limits

  • The map reflects only the sources you uploaded and selected.
  • Its branches tend to follow the papers' own section headings, which are not necessarily the themes of your argument.
  • The built-in map cannot be edited, and the download is an image. Third-party browser extensions claim to export maps to other formats; check what data they access before installing one.
  • Google notes that the mobile app does not support mind maps at present, so use a computer.
