Writing Chapter 2: the literature review

These lessons cover the literature review chapter of an economics PhD from start to finish: finding and judging sources, reading them well, spotting the gap, choosing a framework, and writing a chapter your examiners will respect. Each lesson has notes, worked examples, tools you can use on your own topic, and exercises with model answers.

What a good Chapter 2 does

A good literature review is an argument. It does not list who said what. It shows what is known about your question, how we know it, where the evidence is weak or disputed, and why your study is the sensible next step. Every lesson below serves that one aim.

What a literature review is, and why we write one

Before learning how to search and write, be clear about what the chapter is for. Most weak reviews come from a wrong idea of the task, not from weak English.

After this part you can

  • define a literature review in one sentence of your own;
  • explain the difference between a review and an annotated bibliography;
  • recognise the "product" and the "process" meanings of the term.

A working definition

A literature review is a critical and organised account of what is already known about a research question, written so that the reader can see why a new study is needed and where it fits.

Three words in that definition do most of the work:

  • Critical. You judge the evidence. Which studies are credible? Whose identification strategy is convincing? Where do results disagree, and why?
  • Organised. You arrange the literature by ideas (themes, debates, methods), not by author or by the order in which you happened to read it.
  • Why a new study is needed. The review ends by pointing at something specific that is missing, contested or out of date. That is your gap, and your study fills it.

The questions behind every review are simple ones:

What has been done?

Which questions have been asked, with what data and which methods?

What was found?

Where do findings agree, and where do they conflict?

How good is it?

Strengths and weaknesses of past designs, samples and estimators.

What is left?

What remains unknown, untested, or untested in your setting?

Product and process

The term has two meanings. The product is Chapter 2 itself, the document examiners read. The process is the reading, note-taking and thinking that runs from your first week to the month you submit. The process never really stops: new papers appear while you estimate your models, and the final version of Chapter 2 should reflect them.

Example: one literature, two ways of writing about it

Consider the debate on minimum wages and employment. An annotated-bibliography treatment would say: "Card and Krueger (1994) studied fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Neumark and Wascher (2000) re-examined the data. Cengiz et al. (2019) used a bunching estimator." Each sentence is accurate, and together they say almost nothing.

A review treatment says: "Evidence on the employment effect of minimum wages divides along methodological lines. Case studies that compare neighbouring regions find small or negligible job losses (Card & Krueger, 1994; Dube et al., 2010), whereas studies using payroll records and broader panels have reported negative effects for low-skilled youth (Neumark & Wascher, 2000). More recent work that tracks the whole wage distribution suggests that the number of low-wage jobs changes little after moderate increases (Cengiz et al., 2019). The disagreement is therefore less about the sign of the effect than about which comparison group is credible, and this matters for Malaysia, where the national minimum wage introduced in 2013 took effect across the whole country at once, leaving no untreated region to compare with."

The second version has the same sources but a point of view, a reason the debate exists, and a link to a new study.

Key takeaways

  • A review is an argument that leads to your research questions.
  • Organise by ideas, never by authors.
  • "Critical" means weighing evidence, not criticising people.
Teaching note: a five-minute opener

Show only the annotated-bibliography version of the minimum wage example and ask: "What does this paragraph want me to believe?" Students usually struggle to answer. Then show the review version and ask the same question. The contrast does the teaching.

Which one is Chapter 2?

In most economics PhD theses, Chapter 2 is a thematic narrative review with a documented search. You choose and organise the literature as an expert would, but you also record where and how you searched, so that an examiner can see you did not simply cite what came to hand. Some theses go further and include a full systematic review or meta-analysis as one chapter; that is a methodological choice to agree with your supervisor.

Example from your lecturer's own work

Soon and Ahmad (2015) is a meta-analysis of household willingness to pay for renewable energy. A random-effects model gave a summary estimate of about USD 7.16 per month, and a meta-regression showed that urban and North American households were willing to pay more than rural and Asian ones. The reference list of a meta-analysis like this one is also an excellent starting point for snowballing, because the authors have already searched systematically.

Review articles you should read early

  • Journal of Economic Literature: long surveys by leading researchers. If one exists on your topic, read it first.
  • Journal of Economic Perspectives: shorter, accessible symposia on current debates.
  • Journal of Economic Surveys: reviews and meta-analyses across applied fields.
  • Annual Review of Economics: concise state-of-the-art reviews.

Key takeaways

  • Know which kind of review you are writing; it decides how much search documentation you need.
  • A recent survey or meta-analysis on your topic is the best first read you can find.

Why we write a literature review

Seven jobs the chapter does for you, your examiners and your later chapters.

  1. Avoid reinventing the wheel. Find out what has already been answered, so that your contribution is new.
  2. Find the important variables. The literature tells you which determinants matter, how they are measured, and which controls examiners will expect.
  3. Learn from the strengths and weaknesses of past studies. Which data, estimators and identification strategies worked? Which were criticised, and why?
  4. Find the gap. Something not yet done; something done elsewhere but not in a different or unusual setting; or something done in a way you can improve.
  5. Choose and justify your theory. The review shows which theoretical lens explains your problem and leads to your framework and hypotheses.
  6. Set the benchmark for your results. In your discussion chapter you will compare your estimates with earlier ones. The review provides the yardstick.
  7. Earn credibility. Examiners judge whether you know your field. Boote and Beile (2005) argue that a researcher has to be a scholar of the field first, and Holbrook et al. (2007) found that examiners read the review closely as evidence of the candidate's command of it.

Worked example: what the review decides for a study

Suppose your provisional question is: Did the national minimum wage affect youth employment in Malaysia? A careful review answers several design questions before you touch any data:

Design decisionWhat the literature tells you
Key variableUse the "bite" of the minimum wage (minimum wage relative to the local median wage), not its ringgit level, because the same wage binds more in poorer states.
MethodCompare states or districts where the bite differed, before and after 2013 (a difference-in-differences design), and test whether trends were parallel before the policy.
DataHousehold labour force microdata (DOSM) for employment and wages; check whether informal work is captured.
Controls and threatsRegional trends, sectoral composition, and the spillover of wages to workers just above the minimum.
BenchmarkPublished employment elasticities with respect to the minimum wage, so that your estimate can be compared in Chapter 5.

None of these choices is yours to invent. Each one is justified by citing the literature in Chapter 2.

Try it on your topic

Write your provisional research question at the top of a page. Below it, list the five design decisions above and, next to each, one paper you would cite to justify your choice. Any blank row tells you what to read next.

Discussion question: "My topic is new, so there is no literature." Is this ever true?

Almost never. A topic can be new in its exact form (a new policy, a new dataset), but the underlying mechanisms have literatures. A study of the effect of a new e-wallet subsidy on spending draws on the literature on cash transfers, mental accounting, liquidity constraints and digital payments. The claim "there is no literature" usually means the search was too narrow. Examiners treat it as a warning sign.

Common mistake

Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 tell the same story twice. If your problem statement already summarises ten studies, Chapter 2 has nothing left to add. Keep Chapter 1 to the problem and the gap in brief; let Chapter 2 carry the evidence.

What about a thesis by publication (three-paper format)?

Each paper has its own short literature section, so the thesis-level review is often shorter and sits in an extended introduction. It still has to show how the three papers relate to one body of work and what the thesis as a whole contributes. Check your university's current guidelines on publication-based theses before planning this format, and agree the structure with your supervisor early.

Searching the literature

A good search is planned, recorded and repeatable. This lesson takes you from a research question to a documented search across Google Scholar, subject databases, reference lists and AI tools.

From question to concepts

Search engines do not understand questions; they match words. So the first step is to take your research question apart into its main concepts, then list the different words authors use for each one.

The Journal of Economic Literature classification codes describe what a paper is about, regardless of the words the authors chose. EconLit lets you search by JEL code, and most economics journals print the codes under the abstract. Codes for the example above:

Decide the limits before you search

Write your inclusion and exclusion criteria down first. Deciding them afterwards, once you have seen the results, invites you to keep the convenient papers and drop the awkward ones.

  • Period: for example, 2000 onwards, plus any older seminal work.
  • Document type: peer-reviewed articles, working papers from recognised series (NBER, IZA, CEPR, World Bank), theses, official reports.
  • Language: English and Malay, if you can read both.
  • Evidence type: empirical studies with household-level data; theoretical papers on the mechanism.
  • Exclusions: opinion pieces, conference abstracts without full text, journals that fail your quality check.

Keep a search log

A search log makes your review reproducible and saves you from repeating searches. Examiners increasingly ask how the literature was found.

What Google Scholar cannot do

  • No truncation. remittance* does not find "remittances". Type the variants out with OR.
  • Long queries are cut. Very long strings are truncated without warning; keep each search short and run several.
  • No quality filter. Predatory journals, student papers and duplicates appear next to top journals. You must check every source.
  • Not exactly repeatable. Results and counts change over time. Log the date of every search.
  • At most 1,000 results can be viewed for any search. If you see "About 45,000 results", the search is too broad.
  • The "Cite" button makes mistakes. Its APA references often miss DOIs, issue numbers and correct capitalisation. Always check against the article itself.

5. Follow the citation trail

  • Cited by. Under each result, "Cited by 312" lists later papers that cite it. This is forward snowballing; see Snowballing.
  • Related articles. Papers with similar text and citations. Useful when you have one perfect paper.
  • All versions. Often leads to a free working-paper or repository copy when the journal version is paywalled.
  • Search within citing articles. After clicking "Cited by", tick the box to search only inside the citing papers, for example for "Malaysia".

6. Keep up to date

  • Alerts. Run your best search, then click Create alert (envelope icon) at the bottom of the left panel. New matching papers arrive by email.
  • Citation alerts. On a key paper's "Cited by" page, create an alert to hear about every new paper that cites it.
  • My library. Click the star under a result to save it; label items by chapter section.
  • Google Scholar Metrics. Lists the leading publications in each field (choose Business, Economics & Management, then Economics) ranked by five-year h-index. A useful first look at which journals matter; not a substitute for the checks in the journal quality lesson.

Snowballing: following references backwards and forwards

      
Keyword searches miss papers that use other words. Reference lists and citation links catch them.

    


    

    
The procedure

    
    
      
  1. Choose a start set. Five to ten highly relevant papers, ideally including one recent review or meta-analysis. A recent review is the best seed of all, because its authors have already searched systematically.
    2. 
      
  2. Go backwards. Read the reference list of each paper. For each reference, judge relevance from the title, then the abstract. Add the relevant ones to your library.
    3. 
      
  3. Go forwards. In Google Scholar or Scopus, open "Cited by" for each paper in the start set and screen the citing papers the same way.
    4. 
      
  4. Repeat with the new papers until a round produces nothing new and relevant. This point of saturation is your evidence that the search is complete for that theme. Wohlin (2014) describes this procedure in detail.
    5. 
      
  5. Log it. Record which seed papers you used and how many papers each round added.

A seven-step workflow

    
    
      
  1. Define the question yourself. Write the research question and concept table before opening any AI tool.
    2. 
      
  2. Brainstorm terms with a chat assistant. Ask for synonyms, related JEL codes and alternative phrasings. Use prompt S2 from the library.
    3. 
      
  3. Search a real database semantically. Put your question into Elicit, Consensus or Semantic Scholar. Save the candidate list.
    4. 
      
  4. Verify that every paper exists. Find each one on the publisher's site, through its DOI at doi.org, or in Crossref search. Delete anything you cannot find.
    5. 
      
  5. Check the journal. Run each new journal through the legitimacy scorecard.
    6. 
      
  6. Read the paper. Confirm the finding, the sample and the method in the paper itself. An AI summary is a pointer, not evidence.
    7. 
      
  7. Record the AI use in your search log: tool, date, query, and what you kept. Your university and your examiners may ask.
    8. 
    


    

      
The fabricated reference problem

      
General chat assistants generate text that looks like a reference: a plausible author, a plausible journal, a plausible year. Sometimes the paper exists; sometimes it does not; sometimes it exists but says something else. A single invented reference in a thesis is a serious academic integrity issue, whoever generated it.

      
Treat every AI-suggested reference as unverified until you have opened the paper. See fake citations for the warning signs.

    


    
How to verify a reference in two minutes

    
    
      
  1. Copy the exact title, put it in double quotes, and search Google Scholar.
    2. 
      
  2. If there is a DOI, paste it after https://doi.org/. A real DOI opens the article page.
    3. 
      
  3. Check that authors, year, journal, volume and pages match the article page, not just the title.
    4. 
      
  4. Open the PDF and find the claimed finding. Note the page or table number in your matrix.
    5.

      Discussion: Is it acceptable to start a review from an AI "deep research" report?
      

        
As a map, yes; as a source, no. A deep-research report can show you the shape of a field in an afternoon and suggest search terms and authors. But it is not peer-reviewed, its selection of papers is opaque, and its summaries can be wrong. Use it to plan your own search, then build the review from papers you have found, checked and read. Never cite the report as evidence for a claim about the literature.

When do I stop searching?

    
The honest answer from the lecturer's notes is: almost never. But there are clear signs that the main search for a theme is complete:

    
    
      
  • You have read all the seminal works, the papers everyone in the field cites.
    • 
      
  • New searches and snowballing rounds return only papers you already have.
    • 
      
  • New papers you find cite the ones you have, and add detail rather than new themes.
    • 
      
  • You can explain the main debates in the field without looking at your notes.
    •

  • subscribe to the main journals' table-of-contents alerts or RSS feeds;
    • 
      
  • keep Google Scholar alerts running for your best searches and key papers;
    • 
      
  • join professional associations (for example, the Malaysian Economic Association) and attend their conferences;
    • 
      
  • offer to review for journals in your field: reviewing shows you new work before it is published.

What metrics cannot tell you

      
    
        
  • A journal's impact factor is an average over many articles. A paper in a Q1 journal can still be weak, and a paper in a modest journal can be careful and useful.
    • 
        
  • Quartiles depend on the subject category. A journal can be Q1 in one category and Q3 in another.
    • 
        
  • Metrics differ between fields, so do not compare an economics JIF with a medical one.
    • 
        
  • Indexing is a minimum standard, not a mark of excellence. It tells you a journal passed a review; it does not tell you every article is sound.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Key takeaways

      
    
        
  • Check indexing in the official lists (Master Journal List, Scopus Sources), never on the journal's own website.
    • 
        
  • Combine a general index with an economics ranking (ABDC, AJG or RePEc).
    • 
        
  • Judge the article as well as the journal.

Predatory and hijacked journals

      
Some "journals" exist mainly to collect publication fees. Their articles have not been properly reviewed, and citing them weakens your chapter.

    


    
An international group of researchers and publishers (Grudniewicz et al., 2019) proposed a consensus definition: predatory journals put their own financial interest ahead of scholarship, and can be recognised by false or misleading information, departures from good editorial practice, lack of transparency, and aggressive, indiscriminate solicitation of authors.

    
A related problem is the hijacked journal: a fake website that copies the name, ISSN and look of a real, indexed journal and takes authors' fees. Retraction Watch maintains a Hijacked Journal Checker listing more than 400 of them.


    
Red flags

    

      

        
How they contact you

        
    
          
  • Unsolicited, flattering emails ("Dear Esteemed Professor") inviting papers on any subject.
    • 
          
  • Promises of peer review in days and publication within a week or two.
    • 
          
  • Invitations to join the editorial board after a single email.
    • 
        

      

      

        
What they claim

        
    
          
  • Invented metrics such as a "Global Impact Factor" or "Universal Impact Factor".
    • 
          
  • "Indexed in Scopus / ISI" when the title is not in the official lists.
    • 
          
  • "International Scientific Indexing (ISI)", a name chosen to be confused with Clarivate's former Institute for Scientific Information.
    • 
          
  • Indexing in Google Scholar presented as a quality mark. It is not: Scholar indexes almost anything.
    • 
        

      

      

        
How they look

        
    
          
  • A very broad scope ("economics, management, engineering and medical sciences").
    • 
          
  • A name close to a famous journal, often with "International", "Global" or "American".
    • 
          
  • Editors who cannot be traced, or who do not know they are listed; generic email addresses.
    • 
          
  • An ISSN that is not registered, or belongs to another title.
    • 
        

      

      

        
What they publish

        
    
          
  • Dozens of papers per issue on unrelated topics.
    • 
          
  • Weak English, no peer-review dates, missing or broken DOIs.
    • 
          
  • Literature reviews written author by author, as in the bad examples.
    • 
          
  • Fees revealed only after acceptance, or paid into a personal account.
    • 
        

      

    


    

      
Open access is not the same as predatory

      
Many excellent journals are open access and charge an article processing charge (APC). Paying to publish is not the problem; publishing without real peer review is. Listing in DOAJ, transparent fees, named editors and a clear review process are good signs.

    


    
The grey zone

    
Not every weak journal is predatory. Some are legitimate but have low standards; some were once indexed and later discontinued by Scopus or Web of Science after re-evaluation. Articles a journal published while it was indexed usually stay in Scopus, but a discontinuation is a reason to read those articles more critically. Judge such papers one by one, and prefer stronger sources for any claim your argument depends on.


    

      Teaching note: use students' own inboxes

Analyse two or three in class using the red flags above, then run the journals through the scorecard. Do not name or shame the journals in recorded sessions or slides; focus on the evidence.

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
The ten-minute journal check

      
Do this for every journal you have not seen before. Record the result in your matrix.

    


    
    
      
  1. Find the journal in the official lists. Search the exact title and ISSN in the Clarivate Master Journal List and Scopus Sources. Note which index and which years of coverage.
    2. 
      
  2. Check that the website is the real one. Compare the web address with the one shown in the Master Journal List or Scopus, and search the title in the Retraction Watch Hijacked Journal Checker.
    3. 
      
  3. Check whether it was discontinued. Scopus publishes a list of discontinued titles; see Elsevier's content policy and selection page. If the journal is there, note the year.
    4. 
      
  4. Check the ISSN. Look it up on the ISSN Portal. The title registered must match.
    5. 
      
  5. Look at its rank in economics. SJR quartile on SCImago, ABDC rating, or RePEc ranking.
    6. 
      
  6. For open-access journals, check DOAJ and the fee policy on the journal website.
    7. 
      
  7. Read two recent articles. Are there received and accepted dates? Is the methodology described properly? Do the reference lists look like real scholarship?
    8. 
      
  8. Run the Think. Check. Submit. checklist. thinkchecksubmit.org gives a short checklist used worldwide.
    9. 
      
  9. Check the article itself for retractions. Search the title in the Retraction Watch Database, now openly available through Crossref, and look for corrections on the article page.
    10. 
      
  10. Ask if unsure. Your supervisor and the UUM Library research support team have seen most of the tricks.

    Writing well, and writing badly
    
    
    The same twenty papers can produce a weak review or a strong one. The difference lies in organisation, synthesis and voice. This lesson shows the common mistakes, the approaches that work, and annotated examples from published work.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    After this part you can

      • 
        
    • explain exactly why each one fails;
      • 
        
    • rewrite a weak passage into a synthesis.

    Three ways not to write a literature review
    
    
    The three examples below are re-creations. Each copies the structure of a published "review of literature" that the lecturer has used in class, moved onto an economics topic. All authors cited inside them are fictional (†). The originals are not named, because the point is the pattern, not the people.
    

    
    1. The numbered summary list
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Weak: one numbered paragraph per paper
    
        
    
          
    4. REVIEW OF LITERATURE:
    
          
    4.1. (Rahman, 2015†) had given the more elaborate definition of financial inclusion. According to him financial inclusion is the access of households to bank accounts, credit, insurance and payment services at an affordable cost, first by opening accounts, second by using them regularly, third by ...
    
          
    4.2. Lee & Wong, (2017†) identified the possible areas of difference between financial inclusion in urban and rural areas. The different areas are on the basis of the distance to bank branches, the cost of transactions, the role of mobile money, ... They tried to stimulate further scholarly exploration of the exceptionally complex issues.
    
          
    4.3. Ibrahim, Tan, & D Kumar, (2018†) identified the determinants of financial inclusion. Those determinants include Internal Factors (Income & Education, Age), External Factors (Bank branches, Mobile Network, Regulation), Personal Factors (Trust, Financial Literacy...)
    
        
    
        
    
          What is wrong
          
      
            
    • Organised by author, not by idea: the reader must do the synthesis.
      • 
            
    • No comparison: nothing says whether studies 4.2 and 4.3 agree.
      • 
            
    • No evaluation of data, method or credibility.
      • 
            
    • Citation errors: the first author is in brackets as the subject, and there are stray commas before the year.
      • 
            
    • Long lists copied from the sources, with phrases such as "exceptionally complex issues" that say nothing.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Stronger: one theme, several studies, a judgement
    
        
    
          
    Studies of financial inclusion now distinguish access from use. Having an account is no longer the main constraint in many middle-income countries; regular use is (Rahman, 2015†). Two explanations dominate. Supply-side studies stress the distance to branches and the cost of transactions, and find that inclusion rises when mobile money lowers these costs (Lee & Wong, 2017†). Demand-side studies point instead to financial literacy and trust (Ibrahim et al., 2018†). The two literatures rarely test each other's variables: supply-side studies seldom control for literacy, and demand-side surveys seldom measure distance. As a result, the relative importance of the two constraints remains unknown, which matters for policy because they call for different instruments.
    
        
    
        
    
          Why it works
          
      
            
    • Opens with a claim about the literature, not about one author.
      • 
            
    • Groups studies into two explanations and compares them.
      • 
            
    • Identifies a weakness shared by both groups, which becomes a gap.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    2. The definition dump
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Weak: a list of definitions without sources or purpose
    
        
    
          
    2. Literature Review
    
          
    Green growth is highly regarded, but rarely defined. The most important definitions are as follows:
    
          
    a. Green growth is a concept that encompasses all the economic growth and the interactions between environment and them.
    b. Green growth including clean energy is differentiated from both brown growth and degrowth.
    c. One agency defines green growth as a set of policies, technologies and investments which brings competitive advantage to the country.
    
          
    As a result, measurement of green growth is important for two reasons: first, internal aspect with the goal of the most efficient dedication of resources while minimizing the costs. Second, external reason ...
    
        
    
        
    
          What is wrong
          
      
            
    • Definitions with no citations: whose definitions are these?
      • 
            
    • No comparison of how the definitions differ, or which one the study adopts and why.
      • 
            
    • "As a result" claims a logical link that does not exist.
      • 
            
    • The section is called a literature review but reviews no research.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Stronger: compare definitions, then choose one
    
        
    
          
    Definitions of green growth differ mainly in what they hold constant. A "strong" definition requires output to grow while total emissions fall in absolute terms (Ahmad, 2016†); a "weak" definition only requires emissions per unit of output to fall (Chen & Noor, 2019†). The choice is not semantic. Under the weak definition, most ASEAN economies have achieved green growth since 2010; under the strong definition, almost none has (Chen & Noor, 2019†). Because this study asks whether Malaysia's growth has been compatible with its emission targets, it adopts the strong definition and measures it by absolute decoupling of GDP and carbon dioxide emissions.
    
        
    
        
    
          Why it works
          
      
            
    • Every definition is attributed.
      • 
            
    • Shows why the difference between definitions matters for findings.
      • 
            
    • Ends with the definition adopted and its measurement.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    3. The bold-name laundry list
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Weak: counting studies, then listing them
    
        
    
          
    Research Gap In The Literature
    
          
    The literature review is a significant step in each and every research process. The researcher has reviewed 68 studies, which include 50 Malaysian journals and 18 international journals. The various studies related to household saving have been conducted by different social scientists at micro as well as macro level in Malaysia and abroad. But no study was made on saving behaviour of e-hailing drivers.
    
          
    Daud and Lim, (2016)† have conducted "Determinants of Household Saving in Malaysia". In this study, saving of households in 3 states are investigated. Age is found to be a significant predictor ...
    
          
    Kamal Y. and Siva, (2015)† have identified "An Evaluation of Retirement Awareness Among Youth". This paper deals with the extent ...
    
        
    
        
    
          What is wrong
          
      
            
    • Counting studies (and their journals' nationality) is not evidence of anything.
      • 
            
    • The gap is announced before any evidence is reviewed.
      • 
            
    • "No study on group X" is not, on its own, a reason to study group X.
      • 
            
    • Each paragraph starts with a bold author name and a title in quotes: a bibliography, not a review.
      • 
            
    • Verbs such as "have conducted" and "have identified" are used for everything.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Stronger: a reason why the gap matters
    
        
    
          
    Life-cycle models predict that households save during working years to smooth consumption in retirement, and Malaysian evidence broadly supports the age profile of saving (Daud & Lim, 2016†). These studies, however, rely on samples of salaried employees, whose saving is partly automatic through compulsory pension contributions. Gig workers such as e-hailing drivers face the same retirement problem without automatic saving and with volatile income, two conditions that theory predicts will change saving behaviour (Kamal & Siva, 2015†). Yet no study has measured how much such workers save, or why. The gap matters because the share of workers outside the formal pension system is growing.
    
        
    
        
    
          Why it works
          
      
            
    • Starts from theory, then evidence, then a limitation of that evidence.
      • 
            
    • Explains why the new group should behave differently: that is what makes the gap worth filling.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    Other common mistakes
    
    
    
      
    
        
    MistakeWhat it looks likeThe fix
    
        
          
    Quotation patchworkEvery second sentence is a direct quotation.Paraphrase and synthesise; quote only when the exact words matter, with a page number.
    
          
    Description without evaluationWhat each study found, never how well it was done.Add one sentence of judgement per group of studies: data, identification, sample.
    
          
    Out of dateThe newest citation is eight years old.Run a final search for the last three years before submitting.
    
          
    Missing seminal workA chapter on crime with no Becker (1968).Snowball backwards until the founding papers appear.
    
          
    Cherry-pickingOnly studies that support the hypothesis.Report contrary evidence and explain why results differ.
    
          
    Second-hand citingCiting a 1960s paper you only read about in a textbook.Read the original, or cite it "as cited in" and list only the source you read.
    
          
    Topic tourismPages on the history of Malaysian banking in a study of mobile wallet adoption.Keep only what bears on your research questions.
    
          
    PatchwritingA source's sentences with a few words changed.Close the paper, write from your notes, then check.
    
          
    AI-flavoured filler"In today's rapidly evolving landscape, financial inclusion plays a pivotal role ..."See Spotting AI-written text.

    Ways of organising a review that work

    Anatomy of a synthesis paragraph
    
      
    Claim, evidence, analysis, link. Four moves that turn a summary into an argument.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    MoveWhat it doesTypical wording
    
        
          
    ClaimStates what the literature shows, as your own sentence."Evidence on X has converged on ..." / "Studies of X divide into two groups."
    
          
    EvidenceCites several studies together, grouped by what they share."(A, 2014; B, 2016)" / "Using panel data from ..., A (2014) and B (2016) both find ..."
    
          
    AnalysisCompares, explains differences, judges credibility."The difference appears to reflect ..." / "Because these studies rely on ..., they cannot ..."
    
          
    LinkSays what this means for your study, or leads to the next paragraph."This leaves open whether ..." / "A second strand of research ..."
    
            
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Worked example: a paragraph on mobile money and household risk
    
      
    
        
    Claim Mobile money appears to help households cope with income shocks, mainly by making it cheaper to receive transfers from relatives. Evidence In Kenya, households using M-Pesa maintained consumption after a negative shock, whereas non-users reduced it (Jack & Suri, 2014). In Tanzania, consumption after rainfall shocks was protected for mobile-money users, which Riley (2018) attributes to remittances received through the service. In rural Uganda, users received larger and more frequent remittances and had higher consumption than non-users (Munyegera & Matsumoto, 2016). Analysis All three studies rely on household panels from East Africa and on variation in who adopted mobile money, which may be correlated with unobserved advantages despite the authors' controls. They also come from economies where mobile money spread before formal banking, so the effects may be larger than in countries with dense bank networks. Link Whether e-wallets have similar effects in Malaysia, where most households already hold bank accounts, has not yet been tested.

    Exemplar 1: a debate between two schools of thought
    
    
    
      
    Excerpt from Soon (2010), PhD thesis, University of Otago, Section 2.3 "Early brain drain debates". Citation format as in the original thesis, before APA 7.
    
      
    
        
    The brain drain debate can be traced back to the 1960s when the 'brain drain' term was coined following the mass exodus of scientists from Europe to the United States. The brain drain phenomenon has generated a series of intellectual debates, generally concerning the effects of brain drain on the home (or source/sending) country. There are two schools of thought, the internationalist and nationalist.
    
        
    Note 1
    The opening paragraph gives the origin of the debate and then announces the structure: two schools of thought. The reader now knows how the section is organised.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    The strongest proponents of the internationalist school of thought are Grubel and Scott (1966) and Johnson (1967) who advocated free movement of people across national borders. Migration reflects the free choices on the part of the migrants and is expected to raise total world output. The international circulation of human capital is seen as a beneficial process, unless the private gains from the migration are obtained at a social cost (Johnson 1967, p. 383).
    
        
    Note 2
    The topic sentence names the school first and its proponents second. The idea leads; the authors support it.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Johnson (1967, p. 384) went on to specify that the social cost is insufficient to demonstrate a loss to those left behind (TLB) or the non-migrants in the home country. In order to be considered a net loss, the social cost should exceed the private gains of the migrants plus any gains (or minus any losses) to the host country. Using positive externalities from basic research as an example, Johnson (1967, p. 386) argued that a net social cost (or a loss of world economic welfare) is unlikely to occur from skilled migration because the positive externalities generally outweigh the negative.
    
        
    According to Grubel and Scott (1966, p. 270), skilled migration should be [ ... ] that two conditions are met, i.e., (i) the migrant improves his own income and (ii) a migrant's departure does not reduce the income of TLB in the home country. The first condition is met when the migrant moves as a result of his own voluntary and rational choice. This is because an individual is assumed to behave in a rational welfare-maximizing way. The second condition is met in a perfectly competitive market where wage is paid according to the marginal product of labour, i.e., when an individual migrates, he removes both his contribution to output and his income so that the income of TLB remains unchanged.
    
        
    Lecturer
    These 2 paragraphs explain the 2 theories in detail, separately.
    "Went on to specify" shows the argument developing within one author's work. Page numbers locate each claim precisely.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Hence, in Grubel and Scott's perfectly competitive world where individuals are paid according to their marginal product of labour, there will be no welfare reduction either to the world or to the home country. In other words, the internationalists believe that the welfare of TLB in the home country would only be reduced if the migrants' marginal products or contribution to the national output were higher than the migrants' income (Blomqvist 1986, p. 165).
    
        
    Note 3
    "In other words" restates the position of the whole school in one sentence: synthesis after detail.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    From within the same internationalist school of thought, Berry and Soligo (1969) however, disagreed with Grubel and Scott's marginal flow of skilled people, and had instead argued that for non-marginal flows of skilled migration, the welfare of TLB would generally be reduced. Berry and Soligo's (1969, p. 792) main conclusion was that skilled migration in general would cause welfare reduction to TLB as a whole, with two exceptional cases. The welfare of TLB will not be reduced if in the first case, the migrants own a relatively large proportion of capital stock and in the second case, the migrants leave some or all of their capital behind.
    
        
    Note 4
    Disagreement inside one school is shown, not hidden. The reader sees that the debate is not simply two camps.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    However, to the extent that skilled migration can help relieve some pressures off tight labour markets in the home countries, then such a migration may not be welfare-reducing but may help in the human resource allocation. This situation can be deemed more of an overflow than a brain drain. This is the so-called 'overflow hypothesis' put forward by Baldwin (1970). Baldwin's overflow hypothesis is similar to Lewis's (1954) seminal theory of labour surplus in the agricultural sector migrating to the urban sector. In Lewis's two-sector economy, the move of the surplus labour from the agricultural to the urban sector does not have any adverse effect on the agricultural sector since the marginal product of labour is zero in that sector.
    
        
    Note 5
    Connecting a newer idea to a seminal theory shows command of the field and helps the reader understand it.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    From the nationalist's point of view however, Grubel and Scott's purported frictional costs appear to be too large and harmful to the source country as well as too prolonged to be merely frictional (Watanabe 1969, p. 410). This is because, to nationalist Patinkin (1968, p. 103), the loss involved in the emigration of outstanding skilled manpower does not last only until they have been replaced; there may be a long replacement period that can extend over many years and furthermore, there may be a shortage of such outstanding skilled manpower.
    
        
    Patinkin also disagreed with Grubel and Scott's claim that there is a negligible income redistribution effect due to the small number involved in skilled migration. To Patinkin, the question of redistributing income equally is almost impossible as there is no world government that can do that on an international basis (Patinkin 1968, p. 101). The nationalists maintained that skilled migration will reduce the home country's output by the amount of the migrants' contribution. From the nationalists' narrow point of view, the economic welfare of a country is weighed in terms only of the welfare of its residents, excluding both (i) the welfare of its citizens who have migrated and, (ii) the welfare of the outside world (Johnson 1967, p. 379).
    
        
    Note 6
    The turn to the second school is signposted in the first words of the paragraph, and the nationalist case is built as a direct reply to the internationalist one ("Grubel and Scott's purported frictional costs").
    
      
    
    
    

    
    Exemplar 2: Malaysian studies, and limiting the international review
    
    
    
      
    Excerpts from Soon, Kamaruddin and Anuar (2018), "Flood victims' evacuation decisions: A semi-nonparametric estimation", International Journal of Emergency Services, 7(2), 134–146.
    
      
    
        
    There are only a handful of studies, in the Malaysian context, related to the current study. Padlee and Nik Razali (2015) studied flood victims' satisfaction with services provided at evacuation centres in the east-coast states of Kelantan, Pahang, and Terengganu. They concluded that flood victims were generally satisfied with the quality of services provided, especially in terms of food preparation and medical supplies. In another Malaysian study, Raman et al. (2015) studied how to best manage flood disasters from the perspective of flood victims. Specifically, their study proposed a disaster risk reduction model and recommended ways to manage and cope with flood disasters. Based on their proposed model, the motivation for preparedness in facing flood disasters should come from the people themselves, while rescue agencies and emergency services should focus primarily on ensuring the safety of vulnerable segments of communities and mechanisms for information dissemination. In fact, for disaster information dissemination and management Malaysia has already put in place a web-based integrated community emergency management and awareness system, although the system is still at the prototype stage (Dorasamy et al., 2017; Skar et al., 2016; Raman et al., 2014). Rahman's (2012) work represents a qualitative study on how knowledge of the NSC's Directive 20 amongst various disaster-related agencies in Malaysia's state of Kedah could translate into preparedness in the event of actual disasters. He came to the conclusion that knowledge of the directive did indeed translate into better preparedness for disasters, not only in the event of floods but also landslides, tsunamis, industrial pollution, and droughts. All the aforementioned Malaysian studies are done in the context of floods of typical magnitude.
    
        
    Lecturer
    This para is under the theme "Malaysian studies".
    "A handful" means only 3 studies.
    The last sentence in the para sums up the studies' common feature, which is also the gap: none studied floods of unusual magnitude.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Flood disasters in Malaysia are distinctly different from the natural disasters in some other geographical regions. In the USA for example, flood-related disasters are usually caused by hurricanes. In Malaysia, floods are often caused by heavy incessant tropical rains. Since the international literature is replete with studies on natural disasters, only relatively recent studies (both on actual behaviour and on hypothetical expected behaviour) of the determinants of evacuation decisions or behaviour at the household level are reviewed here. Thompson et al. (2017) provided an excellent systematic review of the much broader international literature concerning evacuation from natural disasters.
    
        
    Lecturer
    This para links Malaysian studies with those from other countries, limits the scope of the review of international studies, and refers readers elsewhere if they want to know more.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Solis et al. (2010) and Mesa-Arango et al. (2013) studied evacuation choices of hurricane disaster victims in the USA. Using a simple probit model, Solis et al. concluded that households affected by hurricanes were more likely to evacuate than to stay if: they were living in mobile homes, they were in flood-prone areas, they had experienced some form of hurricane disaster threat in the past, and they had young children. Mesa-Arango et al., on the other hand, examined types of evacuation destination using a nested logit model, rather than analysing the binary yes-no evacuation decision. Low-income households were found to be more likely to evacuate to public shelters; households that received evacuation notices in advance were more inclined to move to hotels; and households that had experienced past hurricanes tended to stay with relatives and friends.
    
        
    Lecturer
    The phrase "on the other hand" links the two studies by contrasting their use of different models.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    [...] Mozumder et al. highlighted how different types of evacuation orders from the authorities result in different evacuation outcomes. Their findings were largely congruent with previous studies (Dixit et al., 2012; Petrolia and Bhattacharjee, 2010; Fu et al., 2007), with victims of natural disasters exhibiting more positive evacuation behaviour if mandatory evacuation orders are issued compared to voluntary evacuation notices.
    
        
    Note
    One study is placed among others: "largely congruent with" signals agreement and lets three further studies be cited in one clause.

    Tense
    
    
      
      
    • Present simple for established knowledge and for what a text argues: "Human capital theory predicts that ..."; "Becker (1968) argues that ...".
      • 
      
    • Past simple for what a specific study did and found: "Jack and Suri (2014) found that ...".
      •

    Hedging: claim exactly as much as the evidence allows
    
    
    
      
    Overclaiming
    These studies prove that remittances increase education spending.
    
      
    Calibrated
    These studies suggest that remittances are associated with higher education spending, although only two address the endogeneity of migration.

    • Summing up: Taken together, these studies indicate ... .

    References versus bibliography
    
    
    
      You cite it  =  it goes in your References.
    
      You only read it  =  it belongs in a Bibliography, which APA does not use.
      APA 7 asks for a reference list: every work cited in the text, and nothing else. Every in-text citation must appear in the list, and every entry in the list must be cited.
    
    

    
    In-text citations
    
    
    
      
    
        
    CaseParentheticalNarrative
    
        
          
    One author(Becker, 1968)Becker (1968)
    
          
    Two authors(Grubel & Scott, 1966)Grubel and Scott (1966)
    
          
    Three or more authors (from the first citation)(Cengiz et al., 2019)Cengiz et al. (2019)
    
          
    Group author, first then later citations(Department of Statistics Malaysia [DOSM], 2023), then (DOSM, 2023)Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM, 2023), then DOSM (2023)
    
          
    Several works, one parenthesis(Card & Krueger, 1994; Dube et al., 2010)Alphabetical order, separated by semicolons
    
          
    Direct quotation(Johnson, 1967, p. 383)Johnson (1967) argued that "..." (p. 383)
    
          
    Secondary source (use sparingly)(Patinkin, 1968, as cited in Soon, 2010)Only Soon (2010) goes in your reference list
    
            
      
    
    
    
    
    Mix the two forms. Narrative citations put the author in focus ("Becker (1968) argues ..."); parenthetical citations keep the idea in focus ("Crime responds to expected punishment (Becker, 1968)"). A good review mostly keeps the ideas in focus.

    Writing the chapter introduction
    
    
    
      
    Template
    
      
    This chapter reviews the literature on [topic] in order to [purpose: establish what is known about X, identify the gap this study addresses, and develop the framework that guides the analysis]. It focuses on [scope: theories / types of evidence / period / regions] and excludes [what and why].

The chapter is organised as follows. Section 2.2 reviews [theories]. Sections 2.3 to 2.5 examine the empirical evidence on [themes]. Section 2.6 considers studies of Malaysia. Section 2.7 identifies the gap in the literature, Section 2.8 presents the [theoretical/conceptual] framework, and Section 2.9 summarises the chapter.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Worked example: an introduction
    
      
    
        
    This chapter reviews the literature on food price changes and household welfare in order to establish what is known about how households at different income levels respond to food inflation, to identify the gap this study addresses, and to develop the framework that guides the empirical analysis. It concentrates on demand-system and welfare studies published since 1980, together with the earlier work on which they rest, and does not review the literature on food supply or agricultural pricing.
    
        
    Section 2.2 reviews the theory of consumer demand and Engel's law. Section 2.3 examines evidence on food expenditure shares across income groups, and Section 2.4 the evidence on price elasticities. Section 2.5 reviews the welfare effects of food price shocks, and Section 2.6 considers Malaysian studies. Section 2.7 states the gap, Section 2.8 presents the theoretical framework, and Section 2.9 summarises the chapter.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    Reviewing topical and empirical works
    
      
    The longest part of the chapter: what has been found about your question, by whom, with what data and methods, and how credibly.
    
    
    

    
    What to report about an empirical study

    Country, period, population.
    
      
    Data
    Survey, panel or administrative data; sample size.
    
      
    Method
    Estimator and identification strategy.

    Worked example: an empirical section (Malaysian studies are fictional, †)
    
      
    
        
    2.5 Welfare effects of food price increases
    
        
    Whether a rise in food prices harms a household depends on whether it is a net buyer or a net seller of food. Deaton (1989) made this point operational: using household survey data from Thailand, he measured each household's net food position and showed that the first-order welfare effect of a price change is proportional to it. Because urban households and landless rural workers are net buyers, the same price increase that benefits surplus farmers reduces their real income.
    
        
    Later studies applied this approach to the food price spikes of 2007 and 2008. Using household data for nine low-income countries, Ivanic and Martin (2008) found that the short-run effects differed widely by commodity and country, but that increases in poverty were much more frequent, and larger, than reductions. First-order measures of this kind, however, ignore the fact that households substitute towards cheaper foods when prices rise. Attanasio et al. (2013) addressed this by estimating a Quadratic Almost Ideal Demand System for rural Mexico, using data collected to evaluate the Oportunidades cash transfer programme; they measured how rising staple prices affected poverty and compared cash transfers with price subsidies as ways of offsetting the loss.
    
        
    The evidence therefore agrees that food price increases tend to raise poverty where many poor households are net buyers, and shows that a full demand system is needed to capture substitution. Most of it comes from low-income, largely agricultural economies, where many households sell food. In an urbanised upper-middle-income country such as Malaysia, almost all B40 households are net buyers, so the welfare effect may be more uniformly negative; but it also depends on substitution possibilities that have rarely been estimated for Malaysian households by income group (Hashim, 2019†; Lim & Chua, 2021†). Section 2.6 reviews this Malaysian evidence.
    
      
    
      
    Deaton (1989), Ivanic and Martin (2008) and Attanasio et al. (2013) are real studies. Hashim (2019) and Lim and Chua (2021) are fictional placeholders for the Malaysian studies a student would cite.

    Reviewing theoretical works
    
      
    Explain the theories that account for your phenomenon well enough that an examiner sees exactly how they lead to your variables and hypotheses.
    
    
    

    
    Six questions to answer for each theory
    
    
      
      
    1. Origin and core proposition. Who proposed it, when, and what does it say in one sentence?
      2. 
      
    2. Assumptions and mechanism. What must be true about agents and markets for the prediction to follow?
      3. 
      
    3. Predictions. What does it imply about the relationship you study? State the expected signs.
      4. 
      
    4. Extensions. How have later authors relaxed the assumptions or added variables?
      5. 
      
    5. Critiques and evidence. Where has the theory been challenged, and how well does it fit the data?
      6. 
      
    6. Relevance. Which part of the theory will you use, and how will it appear in your framework?
      7.

    Worked example: a theory section on migration
    
      
    
        
    2.2 Human capital theory of migration
    
        
    Sjaastad (1962) proposed that migration is best understood as an investment in human capital. An individual moves if the present value of the expected gains from moving exceeds its costs. The gains are mainly higher earnings at the destination; the costs include money costs, such as travel and the earnings forgone while moving and searching for work, and non-money costs, such as the psychological cost of leaving family and familiar surroundings.
    
        
    Several predictions follow. Because the returns accrue over the remaining working life, younger people should be more likely to migrate. Because costs rise with distance, migration should decline with distance. And because the decision compares discounted gains with costs, a wider expected wage differential should increase migration. Harris and Todaro (1970) extended the framework by replacing the actual wage with the expected wage, the destination wage weighted by the probability of finding a job, which explains why rural people continue to move to cities with high unemployment.
    
        
    The theory has been criticised for treating the individual as the decision maker, when migration decisions are often made by families, and for paying little attention to non-economic motives. For the present study of international students' intentions to return home, its main value is that it identifies the variables through which the decision should work: expected earnings in each country, the cost of moving, family ties and age. These variables are carried into the framework in Section 2.9.
    
      
    
      
    All works cited in this example are real: Sjaastad (1962) in the Journal of Political Economy, and Harris and Todaro (1970) in the American Economic Review.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Theory, model, framework: not the same thing
    
      
    A theory is a general explanation (human capital theory). A model is a specific, often formal, representation of it (Becker's offence function). A framework is the structure you build for your own study from one or more theories. The Frameworks lesson treats the last of these in detail.
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    Reviewing Malaysian works
    
      
    For a thesis that focuses on Malaysia, examiners expect you to know the local literature and to explain how Malaysia differs from the settings of the international studies.
    
    
    

    
    Why a separate treatment
    
    
      
      
    • Institutions differ. The EPF, PTPTN, BR1M and STR transfers, minimum wage orders and fuel subsidies shape behaviour in ways that studies from other countries cannot capture.
      • 
      
    • Data differ. Malaysian studies use particular surveys (the Household Income and Expenditure Survey, the Labour Force Survey) with their own definitions, such as the B40, M40 and T20 income groups.
      • 
      
    • It sets up the contextual gap, but only if you explain why the Malaysian context should change the result.
      • 
      
    • Your examiners may have written some of it. Missing an important Malaysian study is noticed.
      • 
    
    

    
    Two ways to place it
    
    
    
      
    
        
    A separate section
    
        
    After the international evidence, one section on Malaysian studies, as in the funnel. Works well when there are few Malaysian studies, or they share a feature that defines the gap (as in Soon et al., 2018: all studied floods of typical magnitude).
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Woven into each theme
    
        
    Each thematic section ends with the Malaysian evidence on that theme. Works well when there are many Malaysian studies, so that each can be compared directly with the international findings.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    Template
    
    
    
      
    Template
    
      
    Opening: how many Malaysian studies there are, and what they have in common (period, data, method, population).

Body: group the studies by finding; compare each group with the international evidence (consistent? larger or smaller effects? why?).

Evaluation: the main limitations shared by the Malaysian studies (e.g. small convenience samples, a single state, cross-sectional data, no treatment of endogeneity).

Context: what is distinctive about Malaysia that could make the international findings a poor guide.

Closing: one sentence naming the feature all the studies share. This sets up the gap.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Worked example (Malaysian studies are fictional, †)
    
      
    
        
    2.6 Malaysian evidence on food spending and prices
    
        
    Five Malaysian studies have estimated how household food spending responds to income and prices, all using rounds of the Household Expenditure Survey. Three estimate Engel curves only (Hashim, 2019†; Rosli & Ong, 2017†; Yusof, 2020†) and report food budget shares that fall with income, as Engel's law predicts; the share for the lowest income quintile is roughly twice that of the highest. Two estimate full demand systems (Lim & Chua, 2021†; Teh, 2022†), and both find that rice and other staples are price-inelastic, whereas meat and fish are price-elastic.
    
        
    These results agree with the international evidence in direction, but they leave two questions open. First, none of the demand-system studies estimates elasticities separately for the B40, M40 and T20 groups, although the policy debate in Malaysia is framed in exactly these terms. Second, all five use a single survey round, so they cannot show whether responses changed after the food price increases of 2022 and 2023.
    
        
    The Malaysian context also differs in ways that matter. Cooking oil, flour and some staples have been subject to price controls, and many B40 households receive cash transfers, so the price changes households face, and their ability to absorb them, differ from the settings of the international studies. All of the existing Malaysian studies, in short, describe average households at a single point in time.

    The literature gap
    
    
    The gap is the hinge between what is known and what your study adds. A convincing gap is specific, evidenced, and worth filling.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    After this part you can
    
      
      
        
    • define a literature gap and apply three tests to it;
      • 
        
    • tell a weak gap from a strong one;
      • 
        
    • explain why "not yet studied in Malaysia" is not enough on its own.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    What a literature gap is
    
    
    A literature gap is a specific question about your topic that existing research has not answered, has answered inconsistently, or has answered in a way that cannot be trusted for your setting. The lecturer's notes list three common sources:
    
    
    
      
    Not done before
    The question has not been asked, or a new policy or event has created a new question.
    
      
    A different or unusual setting
    A study replicated in a new country, population or period where the result could differ.
    
      
    A better way
    Better data, a stronger design or a better measure than earlier studies used.
    
    
    

    
    Three tests every gap must pass
    
    
      
      
    1. It is real. You searched properly (databases, snowballing, Malaysian sources) and can show the gap with your summary matrix.
      2. 
      
    2. It matters. Filling it changes what we believe about a theory, or what a policymaker should do. "Nobody has done it" is not a reason; nobody has measured the saving behaviour of left-handed accountants either.
      3. 
      
    3. You can fill it. Data, method and time are available to you within a PhD.
      4. 
    
    

    
    Weak and strong gaps

    The strong versions show the form of a good gap statement. The facts inside them (for example, the number of earlier studies) are illustrative, not findings from a real search.
    

    
    
      
    The "not done in Malaysia" trap
    
      
    A contextual gap is legitimate only when you explain why the Malaysian context could change the result: different institutions (EPF, price controls, targeted transfers), a different economic structure, different data, or a different population. Without that reason, examiners will ask "so what if it has not been done here?"
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    Types of literature gap, with economics examples
    
      
    Based on the seven-gap taxonomy of Miles (2017), which builds on Müller-Bloch and Kranz (2015), plus two types that are common in economics theses.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        1. Evidence gap: the findings contradict each other
        
    
          
    Definition. Studies have answered the question, but their results conflict when viewed together.
    
          
    Signals in the literature. "Mixed evidence", "inconclusive", "results differ in sign".
    
          
    Economics example. Estimates of the employment effect of minimum wages range from negligible (Card & Krueger, 1994; Cengiz et al., 2019) to clearly negative for young and low-skilled workers (Neumark & Wascher, 2000).
    
          
    Gap statement. "Because estimates of the employment effect of minimum wages conflict, and the conflict appears to stem from the choice of comparison group, this study uses variation in the minimum wage bite across Malaysian districts to test which comparison holds in a middle-income economy."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        2. Knowledge gap: the research does not exist
        
    
          
    Definition. The findings a researcher or policymaker needs simply do not exist, often because the phenomenon or policy is new.
    
          
    Economics example. When a new targeted subsidy replaces a blanket one, there is no evidence yet on who gained and who lost.
    
          
    Gap statement. "Because the shift from blanket to targeted fuel subsidies is recent, no study has yet measured how it changed the transport and food spending of households near the eligibility threshold."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        3. Practical-knowledge conflict gap: practice departs from research
        
    
          
    Definition. What practitioners or policymakers do differs from what research recommends, and the reasons have not been studied.
    
          
    Economics example. Research generally favours targeted cash transfers over general price controls, yet price controls on staples remain common.
    
          
    Gap statement. "Although the literature finds targeted transfers more cost-effective than price controls, the political and administrative reasons governments keep price controls, and the cost of doing so for low-income households, have received little attention in Malaysia."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        4. Methodological gap: the methods used cannot answer the question
        
    
          
    Definition. Existing studies use methods or data that leave the answer biased, imprecise or incomplete.
    
          
    Signals. Only cross-sectional data, no treatment of endogeneity, only one estimator, measures that are poor proxies.
    
          
    Economics example. Malaysian food demand studies estimate Engel curves only, so they cannot measure price substitution (see Methods literature).
    
          
    Gap statement. "Existing Malaysian studies estimate Engel curves, which cannot capture substitution between foods; this study estimates a Quadratic Almost Ideal Demand System by income group."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        5. Empirical gap: a theory's prediction has not been tested
        
    
          
    Definition. A theoretical proposition exists, but it has not been tested with data, or not in the relevant setting.
    
          
    Economics example. The "brain gain" hypothesis predicts that the prospect of emigration raises investment in education at home, so that a source country may gain human capital overall (Mountford, 1997; Beine et al., 2001).
    
          
    Gap statement. "The brain gain hypothesis has been tested mainly with cross-country data; whether the prospect of working abroad raises educational investment among Malaysian students has not been examined with individual-level data."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        6. Theoretical gap: no adequate theory explains the phenomenon
        
    
          
    Definition. Existing theories cannot explain an observed pattern, or theories conflict and no framework reconciles them.
    
          
    Economics example. Human capital theory treats migration as an individual decision, while the new economics of labour migration (Stark & Bloom, 1985) treats it as a household strategy. Neither alone explains why students whose families paid for their degree abroad often plan to return.
    
          
    Gap statement. "Neither the individual human capital model nor the household model of migration accounts for the role of family financing in students' return decisions; this study develops a framework that combines them."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        7. Population gap: a group has been left out
        
    
          
    Definition. A population that could behave differently has been under-researched.
    
          
    Economics example. Studies of retirement saving focus on salaried employees with EPF contributions; gig workers, smallholders and Orang Asli households are rarely studied.
    
          
    Gap statement. "Retirement saving studies in Malaysia sample formal-sector employees whose saving is partly compulsory; the saving behaviour of e-hailing and delivery workers, who have volatile incomes and no automatic contributions, is unknown."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        8. Contextual (geographical) gap: the setting could change the result
        
    
          
    Definition. Evidence comes from settings that differ in ways that matter for the mechanism.
    
          
    Economics example. Evidence that mobile money helps households cope with shocks comes mainly from East Africa, where mobile money spread before banks did.
    
          
    Gap statement. "Because Malaysian households already have wide access to bank accounts, it is unclear whether e-wallets provide the risk-sharing benefits documented in East Africa."
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        9. Temporal or data gap: the evidence is out of date
        
    
          
    Definition. The relationship may have changed since the data used in earlier studies, after a structural break, a crisis or a new survey.
    
          
    Economics example. Household consumption studies based on surveys from before 2020 may not describe behaviour after the pandemic and the food price rises that followed.
    
          
    Gap statement. "All existing estimates use survey rounds from before 2020; whether food demand elasticities changed after the pandemic and the 2022 to 2023 food price increases has not been examined."

    Gap-spotting and problematisation
    
    
    Sandberg and Alvesson (2011) observed that most research questions are built by gap-spotting: finding something missing within an existing body of work. They distinguish three kinds:
    
    
      
      
    • Confusion spotting: competing explanations exist (close to the evidence gap).
      • 
      
    • Neglect spotting: an area is overlooked or lacks empirical support (knowledge, empirical and population gaps).
      • 
      
    • Application spotting: a theory or method is extended to a new area (contextual and methodological gaps).
      • 
    
    
    
    They argue that more interesting research comes from problematisation: identifying and challenging the assumptions on which a literature rests. For example, instead of adding Malaysia to the list of countries where the life-cycle saving model has been tested, one could question its assumption that households can borrow and save freely, when many B40 households are credit-constrained.
    
    
    
      
    For PhD students
    
      
    A well-argued gap-spotting thesis is entirely acceptable at PhD level. Problematisation is more ambitious and riskier. A useful middle path is to spot a gap and then explain which assumption of the existing literature makes it important.

      Four moves: known, missing, why it matters, what this study does.

    Weak
    There is a lack of studies on remittances in Malaysia. Therefore, this study is conducted.
    
      
    Strong
    Although remittances are associated with higher education spending in many developing countries, most of this evidence comes from economies where remittances are a large share of household income. Malaysia is both a sender and a receiver of migrant workers, and remittances form a small share of income for most households, so their effect on education spending could be negligible or concentrated in particular communities. This matters for the design of education support for low-income households. This study therefore uses Household Expenditure Survey data to estimate the effect of remittance receipt on education spending among B40 households.
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Weak
    Previous studies used OLS, which has limitations. This study will use a better method.
    
      
    Strong
    Malaysian studies of the returns to education estimate wage equations by OLS, so their estimates are likely to be biased upwards by unobserved ability. Because these estimates are used to justify public spending on higher education, an unbiased estimate matters.     Theoretical, conceptual, research and analytical frameworks
    
    
    Four terms that students, and some textbooks, use loosely. This lesson defines each one, shows when to use which, and redraws the lecturer's own examples.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    After this part you can
    
      
      
        
    • define the four kinds of framework and the question each answers;
      • 
        
    • recognise each kind in a published thesis;
      • 
        
    • see how they relate to one another.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    Four frameworks, four questions
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Theoretical framework
    
        
    Answers: Why should these things be related?
    
        
    One or more established theories that explain the phenomenon, presented with their key concepts, assumptions and predicted relationships. Borrowed from the literature and applied to your study. In economics it is often written in mathematical form, as in Becker's (1968) model of offending.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Conceptual framework
    
        
    Answers: What exactly does my study link, and in which direction?
    
        
    Your own synthesis of concepts and variables, drawn from theory and from earlier empirical findings, showing the specific relationships your study examines: dependent, independent, mediating, moderating and control variables. Usually a box-and-arrow diagram with hypotheses.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Research framework
    
        
    Answers: How does the whole study proceed?
    
        
    A map of the research process: issue, research questions, objectives, data collection, design, outputs and expected outcomes. Common in grant proposals, evaluations and mixed-methods studies. It shows the plan, not the theory.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Analytical framework
    
        
    Answers: How will the data be analysed to answer each question?
    
        
    The models, estimators, tests and steps that turn data into answers, and how each result maps to an objective: for example, estimate a demand system, derive elasticities, then compute welfare losses by income group. In economics it is where the theoretical framework becomes an estimable specification.

    How they fit together
    
    

    
    
      
    Terms vary between disciplines and universities
    
      
    Some textbooks treat "theoretical" and "conceptual" framework as synonyms; some Malaysian theses call a conceptual diagram a "research framework". What examiners care about is that the diagram and its explanation show the theory, the variables, the relationships and the link to your research questions. Agree the label with your supervisor, define it in your thesis, and use it consistently.

    Situations and the framework that carries the most weight
    
    
    
      
    
        
    If your study ...The framework that does most of the work
    
        
          
    tests the predictions of one established economic model (e.g., the Becker model of crime, the Mincer earnings function)Theoretical, often in mathematical form, leading straight to the estimating equation
    
          
    combines several theories and earlier findings to explain an outcome with many determinantsConceptual, grounded in a theoretical review
    
          
    is a programme evaluation, a mixed-methods project or a funded consultancy with several work packagesResearch framework, supported by a conceptual or analytical framework for each strand
    
          
    makes a methodological contribution (a new index, a new estimator, a new decomposition)Analytical, with the theory that motivates it
    
          
    constructs an index, such as the lecturer's PPUWIConceptual (dimensions and indicators) plus analytical (weighting and aggregation)
    
            
      
    
    
    

    
    If you may use only one framework
    
    
    Some departments or supervisors ask for a single framework diagram. There is no answer that suits every thesis, so the choice is left to you and your supervisor. Here is the case for each.
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Choose the theoretical framework if ...
    
        
    your contribution is testing theory. It keeps the thesis anchored in economics, and in mathematical form it gives you the estimating equation and expected signs directly.
    
        
    You lose: a visible statement of control variables and of variables drawn from empirical rather than theoretical work, unless you add them to the diagram.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Choose the conceptual framework if ...
    
        
    your study draws on several theories and on empirical findings. It is the most common single diagram in Malaysian theses and shows examiners exactly which variables and hypotheses you test.
    
        
    You lose: the theoretical depth, unless the diagram cites its theories (for example, "adapted from Ajzen, 1991") and the text explains them.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Choose the research framework if ...
    
        
    the study has several strands, methods or audiences, as in evaluations and policy projects. It shows how everything fits together and is easy for non-specialists to follow.
    
        
    You lose: the theory. On its own, a research framework rarely satisfies an examiner who asks "what is your theoretical basis?"
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Choose the analytical framework if ...
    
        
    the contribution lies in modelling and estimation. It shows the path from data to each answer with full transparency.
    
        
    You lose: the explanation of why the variables should be related, which then has to be carried entirely by the text of Chapter 2.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Whatever you choose, the single framework must still show
    
      
      
        
    1. the theory or theories it rests on (cited on the diagram);
      2. 
        
    2. the variables, named as you will measure them;
      3. 
        
    3. the direction of each relationship, with hypothesis labels;
      4. 
        
    4. a clear link to each research question.
      5. 
      
    
    
    

    
    
      Teaching note: three questions for the supervisor
      
    
        
      
          
    1. Which label does our school use for the main framework diagram, and where should it appear?
      2. 
          
    2. Do examiners in our school expect hypotheses to be shown on the diagram?
      3. 
          
    3. Is a mathematical model acceptable as the framework, with the diagram as an aid?
      4.

    1. A framework adapted from the theory of planned behaviour
    
    
    Labelled: theoretical frameworkKartina (2016), adapted from Ajzen (1991)
    
    
    
    
      Analysis: what kind of framework is this, really?
      
    
        
    It is labelled a theoretical framework, and it is clearly built on one theory, which it cites. But it names study-specific variables (entrepreneurship education, generic skills, happiness, socio-demographics) and specifies three relationships to be tested. In the terms of this lesson, it is a conceptual framework grounded in the theory of planned behaviour. Many examiners accept the label "theoretical framework" for such diagrams. What makes it strong is the explicit source ("adapted and modified from Ajzen, 1991") and the numbered relationships, which become the hypotheses. The lecturer's note highlights it as the student's contribution to filling the gap: adding variables such as happiness to the original theory.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
    2. A theoretical framework in mathematical form
    
    
    Labelled: theoretical frameworkBecker (1968)
    
    
    
    
      Analysis
      
    A theoretical framework in the strict sense: an established model stated formally, with signed predictions. It shows that a framework need not be a diagram. In a thesis it would be followed by a conceptual or analytical framework that names the measured variables (arrest rate, average sentence, unemployment, income) and the estimating equation.
    
    
    

    
    
    3. Theory, concept, variable: wage determination
    
    
    Labelled: theoretical framework for wage determinationHand-drawn in the lecture notes
    
    
    
    
      Analysis

It is a theoretical framework that also does the work of a conceptual framework, since it ends in measured variables. It corresponds to the section "Theoretical aspects of the selection of explanatory variables" in Soon (2010).     4. Theory, concept, variable: high-skilled migration

      Analysis
      
    Again a theoretical framework that ends in variables. Notice that one theory (Sjaastad's) supplies two concepts, and that one concept (higher education) is justified by two theories.     5. Research framework: the worth of public and private university education

      Analysis
      
    A research framework with a strong conceptual core: the dimensions and indicators of the index are a conceptual framework for measuring "worth". What it does not show is the theory behind the weights (for example, human capital returns or cost-benefit analysis) or how indicators will be aggregated. Those would go in the theoretical and analytical frameworks.

    Diagram form or mathematical form?
    
      
    Both are legitimate in economics. Many strong theses use both: an equation for the theory and a diagram for the variables.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    Diagram form
    
        
      
          
    • Boxes are concepts or variables; arrows are hypothesised effects, pointing from cause to effect.
      • 
          
    • Label arrows H1, H2 ... and show expected signs.
      • 
          
    • Show moderators as arrows that point at another arrow, and mediators as boxes in the middle of a path.
      • 
          
    • Cite the theory on the diagram ("adapted from ...").
      • 
          
    • Keep it to one page, with the same variable names as in Chapter 3.
      • 
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Mathematical form
    
        
      
          
    • Start from the agent's problem (maximise utility or profit subject to constraints).
      • 
          
    • Derive the behavioural function and the signs of its derivatives.
      • 
          
    • Map each argument of the function to a measured variable.
      • 
          
    • Write the estimating equation and the hypotheses on its coefficients.
      • 
          
    • Keep derivations brief in Chapter 2; put long ones in an appendix.
      •

      2.

      3.

      4.

    Two tools that turn a pile of papers into a structure. The matrix lets you compare studies across the same attributes; the mind map shows how the themes connect. Both can be started with Gemini Notebook, and both must be finished by you.

    Two kinds of matrix
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Summary table
    
        
    Rows: studies. Columns: attributes (country, data, method, findings).
    
        
    Appears in the thesis, usually as "Table 2.x: Summary of empirical studies". Shows the reader the evidence at a glance and lets them see patterns in data and methods.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Synthesis matrix
    
        
    Rows: studies. Columns: your themes or sub-questions.
    
        
    A private writing tool. Each cell says what a study contributes to a theme. Read down a column and you have the material for one thematic paragraph.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    Columns for an empirical economics matrix
    
    
    
      
    
        
    ColumnWhat to recordWhy it matters
    
        
          
    Author (year)APA format, linked to ZoteroSorting and citing
    
          
    Country or region; periodWhere and when the data come fromContextual and temporal gaps
    
          
    Data and sampleSurvey or dataset, unit, NComparability and power
    
          
    Dependent variableAs measuredAre studies measuring the same outcome?
    
          
    Key explanatory variablesAs measuredVariable selection for your model
    
          
    Method and identificationEstimator; what makes it causalMethodological gaps; credibility
    
          
    Main findingsDirection and magnitude, with table or page numberSynthesis; benchmark for Chapter 5
    
          
    LimitationsStated by authors, and your own viewGaps
    
          
    Relevance to my studyOne sentence in your own wordsKeeps the review focused
    
          
    Journal checkIndex and quartile, or "failed"Source quality
    
            
      
    
    
    
    
    For theoretical works use: theory or model, authors, core proposition, key assumptions, predictions, critiques, and the variables it implies. For a meta-analysis add effect size, standard error and a quality score.

    Presenting the summary table in your thesis
    
    
      
      
    • Refer to it in the text ("Table 2.3 summarises ...") and draw a conclusion from it; never let the table replace your synthesis.
      • 
      
    • Order rows by theme, then by year, not by whatever order you read the papers.
      • 
      
    • Use landscape pages and a smaller font if needed; long tables can go in an appendix with a short version in the chapter.
      • 
      
    • Keep each cell to a phrase. If a cell needs a paragraph, the paragraph belongs in the text.

    Building a summary table with Gemini Notebook
    
      
    Gemini Notebook is the new name (since July 2026) of Google's NotebookLM. It still lives at notebooklm.google, and it answers only from the sources you upload, with citations.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Why this tool suits the matrix
    
      
    General chatbots summarise papers from memory and may invent details. Gemini Notebook works from the PDFs you give it and links each statement to the passage it came from, so you can check every cell.     Data Table prompt (paste into the customise box)
    
      
    Create a table with one row for each empirical study in my sources. Use exactly these columns:
1. Author(s) and year (APA in-text style, e.g. "Jack & Suri (2014)")
2. Country or region
3. Period of the data
4. Data source and sample size (unit of observation and N)
5. Dependent variable, as the authors measure it
6. Key explanatory variables
7. Estimation method
8. Identification strategy (what the authors rely on to claim a causal effect; write "none stated" if there is none)
9. Main finding, with the size of the effect and the table or page number where it is reported
10. Limitations stated by the authors

Rules:
- Use only information that appears in the sources. If a source does not report something, write "not reported". Do not infer or estimate.
- Keep each cell under 30 words.
- Exclude sources that are reviews, editorials or theoretical papers, and list their titles below the table.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Limits to keep in mind
    
      
      
        
    • It can misread numbers. Regression tables, especially in scanned PDFs, are a common source of errors. Check every coefficient you will cite.
      • 
        
    • It knows only what you upload. A table built from twelve papers is not a review of the field.
      • 
        
    • It does not judge quality. It will summarise a predatory journal's paper as confidently as an AER paper.
      • 
        
    • Copyright and confidentiality. Check that your library's licence terms allow uploading subscribed PDFs to a cloud service, and never upload confidential data or unpublished work you do not own.
      • 
        
    • Features depend on your plan. Google rolled Data Tables out to paid plans first and to free users afterwards; the menus may change. If Data Table is missing, ask for the table in the chat, save the answer as a note, then export the note to Google Docs.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    
      Teaching note: a 20-minute lab
      
    
        
    Give each pair of students the same five open-access PDFs. Half build the matrix by hand from the abstracts and tables; half use Gemini Notebook with the prompt above. Then swap and verify each other's tables against the papers. The discussion that follows (which errors did the tool make, and which did the humans make?) teaches verification better than any warning.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    The literature review mind map
    
      
    A picture of how the themes of your review connect. Draw it early to plan the chapter; redraw it late to check that the chapter still matches.

It is useful for seeing which themes are thin, where theories connect to evidence, and how to order the chapter sections. Click a node to fold or unfold its branch; scroll or pinch to zoom.

    AI prompts for the literature review
    
    
    A good prompt gives an AI tool a role, the context, a precise task, the inputs, firm limits, a format for the answer, and a check to run on itself. This lesson explains that structure and provides eighteen complete prompts for every stage of the review.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    After this part you can

      •

      •

    The seven parts of a complete prompt
    
    
    
      
    
        
    PartWhat it doesExample
    
        
          
    1. RoleSets the expertise and standards the answer should meet"Act as a sceptical external examiner for an economics PhD."
    
          
    2. ContextTells the tool what it cannot know: your topic, setting, data, stage"My thesis asks how B40 households respond to food prices in Malaysia, using HES data."
    
          
    3. TaskOne clear job, broken into numbered steps"1. Classify the gap. 2. Apply three tests. 3. List the five hardest questions."
    
          
    4. InputsThe material to work on, clearly marked"[PASTE GAP STATEMENT]"
    
          
    5. ConstraintsWhat not to do: the guard against invention and ghost-writing"Use only the sources. Do not add citations. Do not rewrite."
    
          
    6. Output formatThe shape of the answer, so that you can use it directly"A table: sentence | source | verdict | quote."
    
          
    7. Self-checkA test the tool applies before answering"Confirm every quote says what the sentence claims."
    
            
      
    
    
    

    
    Weak and strong
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Weak prompt
    
        
    Write a literature review on minimum wage in Malaysia with references.
    
        
    
          What goes wrong
          
      
            
    • Asks the tool to do the student's job, which is an integrity problem.
      • 
            
    • "With references" invites invented citations.
      • 
            
    • No research question, so the review has no direction.
      • 
            
    • No format, no limits, no check.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Strong prompt
    
        
    ROLE: Act as a senior labour economist who writes survey articles.
CONTEXT: My question is whether Malaysia's 2013 minimum wage reduced youth employment. Below is my matrix of 18 studies.
[PASTE MATRIX]
TASK: Propose 3 to 5 themes that organise these studies; assign each study; note agreements, disagreements and likely reasons.
CONSTRAINTS: Use only the studies in the matrix. Do not add studies. Do not write paragraphs.
OUTPUT: One block per theme: name | studies | agreements | disagreements.
SELF-CHECK: Every study appears under at least one theme.
    
        
    
          Why it works
          
      
            
    • The tool organises your material; you write the review.
      • 
            
    • Inputs are your own verified matrix, so nothing is invented.
      • 
            
    • The output can go straight into your planning.
      • 
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    One prompt, dissected
    
    
    
      
    Prompt W4 from the library: "Stress-test my gap statement".
    
      
    
        
    ROLE: Act as a sceptical external examiner for a PhD in applied microeconomics.
    
        
    Role
    "Sceptical" and "examiner" raise the standard of the answer.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    CONTEXT: My research question is "..." in Malaysia. My gap statement is: [PASTE]
    
        
    Context and input
    The tool cannot judge a gap without knowing the question and the setting.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    TASK: 1. Classify the gap ... 2. Apply three tests ... 3. Ask the five hardest questions ... 4. Point out any claim that is too strong.
    
        
    Task
    Numbered steps get numbered answers, which are easy to act on.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    CONSTRAINTS: Do not rewrite the statement and do not invent studies that might already fill the gap; instead, tell me what kind of study I should search for.
    
        
    Constraints
    Keeps authorship with you, and turns a likely hallucination ("Smith (2021) already did this") into a search instruction.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    SELF-CHECK: Make sure the examiner questions are specific to my statement, not generic.
    
        
    Self-check
    Reduces the stock answers that sound wise but fit any thesis.
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Four habits
    
      
      
        
    • Give the tool your material to work on, rather than asking it what it knows.
      • 
        
    • Ask for critique and structure, not for finished prose.
      • 
        
    • Forbid invented citations explicitly, every time.
      • 
        
    • Treat every output as a draft for you to verify.

    Using AI responsibly in Chapter 2
    
      
    You are the author. AI tools may help you search, organise and check; they may not think or write the review for you.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    Generally acceptableAsk first, and discloseNot acceptable
    
        
          
    
            
    • Brainstorming keywords and synonyms
    • Finding candidate papers in tools built on real databases
    • Explaining a method you are learning
    • Organising your own notes and matrix
    • Critique of your draft that you then act on
    		
            
    • Language editing of passages you wrote
    • Summaries of papers you will then read
    • Drafting tables from uploaded sources
    • Translating a source from another language
    		
            
    • Generating paragraphs or sections and presenting them as your own
    • Citing a paper you have not read
    • Including any reference you have not verified
    • Uploading confidential data, unpublished work by others, or examiners' reports
    		
          
    
            
      
    
    
    
    
    This is general guidance. Your university, your graduate school and the journals you submit to set the rules that apply to you. Check UUM's current official guidance on generative AI and ask your supervisor; where rules differ, the stricter rule applies.
    

    
    What journals and publishers expect
    
    
    Publishers broadly agree on two points: an AI tool cannot be listed as an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work (COPE's position statement on authorship and AI tools makes this point), and authors must disclose how they used AI tools, typically in the methods or acknowledgements. Many allow AI-assisted language editing with disclosure. Check the policy of each journal before you submit chapters as papers.
    

    
    A disclosure statement you can adapt
    
    
    
      
    Example disclosure (for the thesis front matter or methodology)
    
      
    Use of generative AI tools. In preparing Chapter 2, I used [tool name and version] between [month year] and [month year] to (a) suggest search terms and JEL codes, (b) draft a first version of the summary matrix from papers I had selected and uploaded, and (c) suggest language edits to passages I had written. I checked every entry in the matrix against the original papers, verified every reference, and accepted or rejected each language edit individually. No text generated by these tools has been included without my revision, and I take full responsibility for the content of this thesis.
    
    
    

    
    Verification is not optional
    
    
      
      
    1. Every reference exists: found on the publisher's site or through its DOI.
      2. 
      
    2. Every reference says what you say it says: checked in the paper, with page or table numbers in your notes.
      3. 
      
    3. Every number is right: coefficients, sample sizes and years copied from the paper, not from a summary.
      4. 
      
    4. Every sentence is yours: you can explain and defend it in your viva.
      5. 
    
    

    
    
      Teaching note: the viva test
      
    
        
    Tell students that any sentence in Chapter 2 may be read back to them in the viva with the question "What did you mean by this, and how do you know?" A sentence they cannot answer for should not be in the thesis, whoever or whatever drafted it. This framing works better than lists of prohibited uses.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

    



    
  
    
    
    Spotting AI-written text
    
    
    "Slop" is text that sounds fluent but says little: inflated words, stock phrases, formulaic structure and, at worst, invented citations. Learn to recognise it in sources, in classmates' drafts, and above all in your own writing.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    After this part you can
    
      
      
        
    • recognise the vocabulary most associated with chatbot prose;
      • 
        
    • replace it with plain, specific words;
      • 
        
    • explain why a single word proves nothing, but a cluster is a warning.
      • 
      
    
    
    

    
    Why certain words give text away
    
    
    Large language models favour some words far more than human writers do. Kobak et al. (2025) analysed more than 15 million biomedical abstracts from 2010 to 2024 and found an abrupt rise, after chatbots became widely available, in "style words" such as delves, showcasing and underscores. From this excess vocabulary they estimated that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts had been processed with a language model.
    

    
    
      
    One word proves nothing
    
      
    People wrote "crucial" and "comprehensive" long before chatbots. What signals generated text is a cluster: several of these words in one paragraph, together with the structural patterns in the next part and an absence of specifics. Use the list to edit your own work, not to accuse others.
    
    
    

    
    The list
    
    
    
    
    
      
    
        
    Word or phraseTypeWrite insteadWhy it is a problem
    
        
      
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    Structural and rhetorical patterns
    
      
    Generated text is recognisable as much by its shape as by its words.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    PatternExampleFix
    
        
          
    Generic opener"In today's rapidly evolving economic landscape, ..."Start with a fact about your topic, with a date and a source.
    
          
    Moralising closerEvery paragraph ends "Overall, this highlights the importance of ..."End with the specific implication, or simply stop.
    
          
    Lists of exactly three"efficient, equitable, and sustainable"; "households, firms, and governments"Use the number of items the content actually has.
    
          
    "Not only ... but also"; "not just X, but Y""Mobile money not only empowers households but also bolsters resilience."Say what it does, with evidence.
    
          
    Uniform rhythmEvery sentence 20 to 25 words; every paragraph five sentences.Vary length naturally; let short sentences carry key points.
    
          
    Mechanical connectorsFurthermore, ... Moreover, ... Additionally, ... at the start of most sentencesLet the logic connect the sentences; use a connector only where it adds meaning.
    
          
    Em-dash habit"Remittances — a vital lifeline — support education — especially for girls."Commas, colons or brackets; or two sentences.
    
          
    Vague attribution"Studies have shown ..."; "Research suggests ..."; "Experts agree ..."Name and cite the studies.
    
          
    False balance"On one hand ... on the other hand ..." with no judgementSay which evidence is stronger, and why.
    
          
    Synonym cycling"financial inclusion ... access to finance ... financial access ..." for the same thingDefine one term and keep it; changing terms suggests a change in meaning.
    
          
    Hedge stacking"may potentially suggest a possible link"One hedge, matched to the strength of the evidence.
    
          
    Bullet points in place of proseA literature review written as bulleted lists with bold lead-insParagraphs: the argument lives in the connections.
    
          
    Headline-style headings"Navigating the Landscape: A Comprehensive Overview of Financial Inclusion"Plain descriptive headings: "2.3 Determinants of account use".
    
          
    Chatbot residue"Certainly! Here is a revised paragraph ..."; "As of my last knowledge update ..."Never paste a chatbot answer into your thesis.
    
            
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    Try it
    
      
    Count the patterns in this paragraph: "In today's world, poverty plays a crucial role in shaping outcomes. Moreover, studies have shown that education is vital. Furthermore, it is not just about income, but about opportunity, dignity, and empowerment. Ultimately, a holistic approach is paramount." Then check your count in the scanner.
    
    
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    Fabricated and garbled citations
    
      
    The most serious trace of careless AI use, because it is a matter of academic integrity, not style.
    
    
    

    
    Warning signs in a reference
    
    
      
      
    • The title sounds exactly right for your argument, but Google Scholar cannot find it in quotation marks.
      • 
      
    • Real, well-known authors, attached to a paper they did not write.
      • 
      
    • A plausible journal that does not publish on that topic, or volume and page numbers that do not match the year.
      • 
      
    • A DOI that does not resolve, or resolves to a different paper.
      • 
      
    • A year in the future, or a "2024" paper cited for data that were not released until later.
      • 
      
    • "et al." on a single-author paper, or the wrong first author.
      • 
      
    • A perfectly formatted reference list alongside messy, inconsistent in-text citations.
      • 
    
    

    
    Warning signs in the text
    
    
      
      
    • A finding attributed to a study that, on reading, says something different or nothing on the point.
      • 
      
    • Summaries with no numbers, samples or methods, only conclusions.
      • 
      
    • No disagreement anywhere in the literature.
      • 
      
    • Missing seminal works, but many recent and obscure ones.
      • 
      
    • Direct quotations without page numbers.
      • 
    
    

    
    
      
    Worked example: checking a suspicious reference
    
      
    A draft cites: Rahman, A., & Lee, K. (2021). Mobile wallets and household resilience in Malaysia. Journal of Development Economics, 152, 102–118.†
    
      
      
        
    1. The exact title in quotation marks returns nothing in Google Scholar.
      2. 
        
    2. The Journal of Development Economics uses article numbers (for example, 102718) rather than page ranges such as 102–118 for recent volumes.
      3. 
        
    3. No DOI is given; Crossref finds no match for the title.
      4. 
        
    4. Conclusion: treat as fabricated. Remove it, and find a real source for the claim or drop the claim.
      5. 
      
    
    
    

    
    See the AI search lesson for the two-minute verification routine, and use prompt C3 in Gemini Notebook to check whether your sentences match the papers you cite.
    
  
    

  
  
    
    
    
      
    What AI detectors can and cannot do
    
      
    Detector scores are probabilities, not proof, and they are least reliable for exactly the writers in a Malaysian PhD class.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    
        
    Limitations
    
        
      
          
    • False positives. Human text is sometimes scored as AI-generated.
      • 
          
    • Bias against second-language writers. Liang et al. (2023) found that several widely used detectors misclassified essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated far more often than essays by native speakers, because simpler, more predictable wording scores as "machine-like".
      • 
          
    • Easy to evade. Paraphrasing tools and light editing change scores substantially.
      • 
          
    • Opaque. A score does not show which sentences are suspect, or why.
      • 
        
    
      
    
      
    
        
    Better evidence
    
        
      
          
    • The writing process: drafts, version history, reading notes, the Zotero library.
      • 
          
    • Verifiable references that the student has read.
      • 
          
    • The student's ability to explain any passage and its sources in conversation.
      • 
          
    • Consistency of voice and quality with the student's other work.
      • 
        
    
      
    
    
    

    
    
      
    For supervisors and examiners
    
      
    Never treat a detector score alone as evidence of misconduct; detector vendors themselves advise against this. If writing seems generated, ask the student to talk you through the passage, show their notes and drafts, and open the cited papers to the relevant page. Follow your university's procedures for academic integrity.
    
    
    

    
    
      
    For students
    
      
      
        
    • Keep your drafts, notes and search log: they are your evidence of authorship.
      • 
        
    • Disclose any AI assistance as your university requires.
      • 
        
    • Write specifically. Specific writing is both better scholarship and less likely to be mistaken for generated text.
      •

      Writing the slop out
      
      
      Eight habits that make your review clearer and unmistakably yours.
      
    
      

    
        
      
      1. Be specific. Numbers, places, periods, sample sizes and names of policies. "Food prices rose by [figure]% in 2022, according to DOSM" says more than "prices are rising".
        2. 
      
      2. Use plain verbs. Use, show, find, examine, estimate. Not leverage, showcase, delve, underscore.
        3. 
      
      3. Cut empty openers and closers. Start with the claim; stop when the point is made.
        4. 
      
      4. Make a judgement. Which evidence is stronger, and why? Fluent neutrality is the mark of generated text.
        5. 
      
      5. Keep one term for one concept, defined once.
        6. 
      
      6. Let rhythm follow meaning. A short sentence after a long one gives weight to the key point.
        7. 
      
      7. Cite precisely, with page numbers for quotations and specific findings.
        8. 
      
      8. Read it aloud. If it does not sound like something you would say to your supervisor, rewrite it.
        9. 
    
      

    
      Before and after
      
    
      
      
      
        
      Sloppy
      
        
      In today's rapidly evolving economic landscape, food security plays a pivotal role in fostering household well-being. Numerous studies have delved into this multifaceted issue, shedding light on its intricate dynamics. Moreover, it is not just about availability, but about access, affordability, and nutrition. Ultimately, a holistic approach is crucial to navigate these complexities.
      
      
      
      
      
        
      Plain and specific
      
        
      Food takes a larger share of spending in poorer households, so food price increases reduce their real income most. Deaton (1989) showed how to measure this effect from household survey data, and Ivanic and Martin (2008) found that the 2007 to 2008 price spikes raised poverty in most of the nine low-income countries they examined. Whether the same holds in Malaysia, where few B40 households produce their own food, has not been tested.
      
      
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
        
      Sloppy
      
        
      This chapter will delve into a comprehensive overview of the literature, showcasing key insights and valuable perspectives that underscore the importance of the topic.
      
      
      
      
      
        
      Plain and specific
      
        
      This chapter reviews theories of consumer demand and the evidence on how food spending responds to prices, and identifies what remains unknown for Malaysian households.

        T8. Write a gap statement
        
      
          
      Scenario. Your matrix holds 26 studies on household saving. Twenty use data on salaried employees. Theory predicts that income volatility increases precautionary saving, but liquidity constraints reduce the ability to save. Malaysia's gig workforce has grown, and most gig workers are not required to contribute to the EPF. You have access to a 2025 survey of 900 e-hailing and delivery workers.
      
          
      Write a gap statement using the four moves: known, missing, why it matters, what this study does.
      
          
      
            Reveal model answer
            
      
              
      
                
      Although the determinants of household saving are well documented, the evidence comes almost entirely from salaried employees whose saving is partly compulsory through EPF contributions. Gig workers differ in two ways that theory predicts will matter, and in opposite directions: their income is more volatile, which should raise precautionary saving, and they lack automatic contributions and often face liquidity constraints, which should reduce it. How these forces balance is unknown. The answer matters because the share of workers outside the compulsory pension system is growing, with implications for old-age poverty. Using a 2025 survey of 900 e-hailing and delivery workers, this study estimates how income volatility and liquidity constraints affect their saving.

      What they mean, and what to do.
      
    
      
    
      
      
      
        
      CommentWhat it usually meansWhat to do
      
        
          
      "Too descriptive."You report what studies found but not what it adds up to or how good it is.Add claim and analysis sentences; see paragraph anatomy.

      Glossary
      
    
      
      
      Annotated bibliography
      A list of sources, each with a short summary. Not a literature review, because it does not synthesise.
      
      
      Backward snowballing
      Finding earlier relevant work through the reference lists of papers you have.
      
      
      Boolean operators
      AND, OR and NOT (in Google Scholar, the minus sign), used to combine search terms.
      
      
      Conceptual framework
      The researcher's own diagram of the concepts and variables a study links, with hypothesised relationships.
      
      
      DOI
      Digital Object Identifier: a permanent identifier for an article, written in APA 7 as https://doi.org/...
      
      
      ESCI
      Emerging Sources Citation Index, part of the Web of Science Core Collection.
      
      
      Evidence gap
      A gap created by conflicting findings.
      
      
      Forward snowballing
      Finding later work that cites a paper you have, through "Cited by".
      
      
      Gap-spotting
      Constructing research questions by finding what is missing in an existing literature (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011).
      
      
      Grey literature
      Research outside journals: working papers, theses, reports by governments and international organisations.
      
      
      Hijacked journal
      A fake website imitating a real, indexed journal.
      
      
      Identification strategy
      What allows a study to interpret an estimate as causal: an experiment, a natural experiment, an instrument, panel variation.
      
      
      Journal Impact Factor
      Clarivate's measure of average citations to a journal's recent articles.
      
      
      JEL codes
      The Journal of Economic Literature subject classification used by economics journals and EconLit.
      
      
      Meta-analysis
      Statistical combination of effect estimates from many studies.
      
      
      Predatory journal
      A publication that puts its own financial interest ahead of scholarship, with misleading claims and no genuine peer review.
      
      
      PRISMA
      A reporting standard for systematic reviews, including the flow diagram of records (Page et al., 2021).
      
      
      Problematisation
      Constructing research questions by challenging the assumptions of existing literature.
      
      
      Quartile (Q1 to Q4)
      A journal's rank within its subject category, by a citation metric.
      
      
      Research framework
      A map of the research process, from issue and questions to data, methods, outputs and outcomes.
      
      
      Synthesis matrix
      A table of studies against themes, used to write thematic paragraphs.
      
      
      Systematic review
      A review that answers a defined question using all eligible studies found by a documented, reproducible search.
      
      
      Theoretical framework
      The established theory or theories that explain why the variables in a study should be related.
      
      
      Truncation
      A symbol (usually *) that finds all endings of a word stem, such as remittance*. Not supported by Google Scholar.
      
    
      
  
      

  
  
      
    
      Frequently asked questions
      
    
      
      
      How many references should Chapter 2 have?
      There is no correct number. Economics theses commonly cite between 100 and 250 works overall, but a smaller set of well-chosen, well-read sources beats a long list. Examiners judge coverage of the important work, not the count.
      
      
      How long should the chapter be?
      Often a fifth to a quarter of the main text. Check your faculty's thesis guidelines and look at recent theses from your school. Length should follow from the number of themes your questions require.
      
      
      Can I cite working papers?
      Yes, from recognised series (NBER, IZA, CEPR, World Bank, central banks). Check whether a published version now exists and cite that instead if so.
      
      
      Can I cite theses and Wikipedia?
      PhD theses, yes, with care; master's theses, sparingly. Wikipedia, no: use it to find sources, then cite those.
      
      
      What if I find a paper that has already done my study?
      Do not panic and do not hide it. Read it closely: different data, period, method or population usually leave room. Discuss it with your supervisor; it may sharpen your gap into a methodological or temporal one.
      
      
      May I use "I" or "we"?
      Economics journals accept "I" for single authors and "we" for co-authors. Some supervisors prefer "this study". Follow your supervisor and be consistent.
      
      
      Should hypotheses be in Chapter 2 or Chapter 3?
      Either, depending on your school. They should come directly after the framework that justifies them.
      
      
      How do I treat sources in Malay?
      Cite them normally. In APA 7, give the original title followed by an English translation in square brackets in the reference list.
      
      
      Can I use AI to paraphrase sources?
      Paraphrasing a source with a tool still leaves you responsible for accuracy and attribution, and it often produces patchwriting. Read, take notes in your own words, and write from your notes. See Responsible use.
